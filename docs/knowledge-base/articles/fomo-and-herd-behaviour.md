# FOMO, Herd Behaviour and Market Bubbles

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain FOMO and herd behaviour, describe how a bubble builds and bursts in general terms, list warning signs, and encourage a pause before acting on excitement, without predicting any market move or naming a current bubble.

## Core Concepts

- **FOMO (fear of missing out)** is the urge to buy something because others appear to be making money from it, even without understanding it.
- **Herd behaviour** is following what the crowd does. When many investors buy because others are buying, prices can rise faster than the underlying value justifies, and the same pattern works in reverse when everyone sells.
- The SEC's bulletin on investor behaviour describes **manias and panics**: "Financial 'mania' or a 'bubble' is the rapid rise in the price of an investment, reflecting a high degree of collective enthusiasm or exuberance regarding the investment's prospects. This rapid rise is usually followed by a contraction in the investment's price. The contraction, or 'panic', occurs when there is wide-scale selling of the investment that causes a sharp decline."
- It also lists **momentum investing**, buying what has recently gone up, as a behaviour that can hurt results.
- SEBI's Financial Education Booklet warns that investors "should ignore the rumours and unsolicited messages for investing in securities market" and says: "Do not rely on making your investment decisions on hot tips as a person who wants to offload securities which may not be marketable may be indulging in it. Disseminating hot tips is also an illegal activity which should be reported to SEBI."
- Market bubbles are usually **recognised only in hindsight**. Exuberance can last longer than expected, and falls can start suddenly.
- **Warning signs** (general educational knowledge): a rapid rise with little change in the underlying business, stories that "this time is different", an investment that is suddenly everywhere on social media, friends who never invested before asking about it, pressure to act before a deadline, and promises of unusually high or steady returns.
- **Social media** can speed up herd behaviour, and influencers may have undisclosed conflicts of interest.
- **Ways to slow down:** ask what the investment is, how it earns money, who benefits if you buy, and what happens if the price halves. Check whether the seller is registered (see "How to Verify a Financial Intermediary"). Decide an amount you could lose without harm before buying anything speculative.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical share, with invented numbers, to show the pattern of a bubble. It is not a forecast of any real security:

| Stage | What happens | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Quiet start | The company reports good news; a few investors notice | ₹100 → ₹150 |
| Crowd arrives | Media and social posts spread; many buy because prices are rising | ₹150 → ₹400 |
| Euphoria | New buyers appear who do not know what the company does | ₹400 → ₹600 |
| Reversal | Good news fades; early buyers sell; others join the selling | ₹600 → ₹250 |

A buyer at ₹600 needs a rise of 140% just to get back to the purchase price. The price rose because buyers kept arriving, not because the business changed in step.

## Misconceptions

- "If everyone is buying it must be safe." Crowds can be wrong, and prices can rise on enthusiasm alone.
- "I'll sell just before it peaks." Peaks are known only afterwards; late buyers often hold when the price falls.
- "FOMO is only about shares." It also affects crypto, property, gold, IPOs and "guaranteed" schemes.
- "Missing out is a loss." Not buying cannot lose money; paying a bubble price can.
