# Safety, Liquidity and Return: The Three Pillars of an Investment

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain safety, liquidity and return, describe why an investment usually gives up one to gain another, and explain liquidity risk and inflation risk, without suggesting which trade-off a user should choose.

## Core Concepts

- SEBI's Financial Education Booklet says an individual's investment decision is influenced by **safety, liquidity and return**, called the three pillars of investment.
- **Safety** is how well the principal and the return are protected, that is, how likely the money is to be repaid when due or needed.
- **Liquidity** is the degree of ease with which an investment can be cashed in at fair value: how soon money comes back if it is needed immediately.
- **Return** is what the investment earns, as income (interest or dividends), as capital appreciation (a rise in market value), or both. If the market value is lower than at purchase, it is a depreciation or loss.
- Investments typically trade these off. The booklet notes that options preferred for liquidity and safety are usually preferred for those reasons rather than for returns, while those with less liquidity usually carry higher risk and return. An investment rarely scores best on all three.
- **Liquidity risk** (SEBI booklet) arises when an investment cannot be bought, sold or converted into cash quickly enough to prevent or limit a loss or to meet an immediate expense. FINRA describes it as how easy or hard it is to cash out when you need to. Locking money in, or having to sell in a falling market, are examples.
- **Inflation risk** (FINRA) is that an investment, such as a bank deposit or certificate of deposit, may not earn enough over time to keep pace with the rising cost of living, so safety of the amount can still mean a loss of purchasing power.
- **Capital preservation** means aiming to keep the amount invested intact. It is usually pursued by choosing options with higher safety and liquidity, and it can conflict with return and inflation.
- The same asset can sit differently on the three pillars depending on how long the money is needed: money needed in a few months values liquidity and safety, money not needed for many years can tolerate more price movement.
- Matching an investment's liquidity to when the money is needed is a planning question; the article describes the trade-offs, not what a person should hold.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical person will need ₹5,00,000 in six months for a known expense.

- A product that locks money for five years has low liquidity for this purpose, whatever its return.
- A product whose value moves daily has price risk: after a fall of 20%, ₹5,00,000 would be ₹4,00,000 when the money is needed.
- A product that returns the amount quickly with little price movement protects the sum and its timing but may earn less than inflation over longer periods.

No option wins on all three; which trade-off fits depends on the person and the date the money is needed.

## Misconceptions

- "The safest option is always best." Safety often comes with lower returns and can lose to inflation over time.
- "A liquid investment is a safe one." Something easy to sell can still fall in price.
- "Higher return means something is wrong." Higher return generally goes with giving up safety or liquidity, but a promised high return with no stated risk is a warning sign.
- "Liquidity only matters in emergencies." It also matters whenever money is needed on a known date.
