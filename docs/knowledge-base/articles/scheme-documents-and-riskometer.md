# Mutual Fund Scheme Documents and the Riskometer

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to name the main mutual fund offer documents, say what each is for, explain the six riskometer levels, and make clear that the riskometer is a label to read alongside the documents, not a promise.

## Core Concepts

- A mutual fund scheme is described in a set of offer documents. The three commonly named are the **Scheme Information Document (SID)**, the **Statement of Additional Information (SAI)** and the **Key Information Memorandum (KIM)**.
- The **SID** sets out concisely the information about a scheme that a prospective investor ought to know before investing. A SEBI-format SID states that the units offered have not been approved or recommended by SEBI, and that SEBI has not certified the accuracy or adequacy of the document. Investors should also check with the fund house, its investor service centres or website for changes made after the document's date.
- The **KIM** is an abridged offer document with the information in concise form. SEBI's mutual fund FAQ says it is required to be given to a prospective investor, and that the application form is an integral part of the offer document.
- The **SAI** carries additional information about the fund house and its operations that is common across schemes. (This description is the usual one; the SAI's contents were not read in a source for this article.)
- **Product labelling** and the **riskometer:** SEBI's FAQ says the risk is depicted through a pictorial meter called the riskometer, with six levels: low, low to moderate, moderate, moderately high, high and very high. Investors can find a scheme's product label on the front page of the initial application form, the KIM, the SID and scheme advertisements.
- SEBI's investor website says the riskometer is mandatory for fund houses to display, that the levels are reviewed periodically, and that fund houses must include it in fact sheets and promotional material. That page's introduction says "five" categories but lists six, so treat the FAQ's "six levels" as the figure to use.
- The riskometer is a summary of risk. It does not say how much an investor will earn or lose, and a "low" label is not a guarantee of capital.
- The standard market-risk warning, that mutual fund investments are subject to market risks and that the scheme documents should be read carefully, appears alongside these labels.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical investor is comparing two schemes before investing.

- Step 1: read each scheme's product label and riskometer level on the KIM or front page of the application form.
- Step 2: read the SID sections on the scheme's objective, where it can invest, the risks it names, the expenses and the exit load.
- Step 3: compare the riskometer level with how much a fall in value the investor could bear, which is a personal question about risk capacity, not something the riskometer answers.

No recommendation follows from the example: the documents describe the scheme, and the choice depends on the investor's own situation.

## Misconceptions

- "SEBI approved this fund, so it is safe." The SID states that SEBI has not certified the accuracy or adequacy of the document or recommended the units.
- "A low riskometer means I cannot lose money." The label is a relative measure of risk, not a guarantee.
- "The KIM is enough." It is an abridged document; the SID gives the full scheme details.
- "The riskometer never changes." SEBI's investor page says levels are reviewed periodically and updated, so the label on an older document may be out of date.
