# Portfolio Rebalancing

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what rebalancing is, why a portfolio drifts away from its target mix, the main ways to rebalance, and the trade-offs (costs and taxes), without telling the user to rebalance or what mix to hold.

## Core Concepts

- A portfolio's **target allocation** is the mix of asset classes an investor has chosen, for example 60% equity and 40% debt. Choosing one depends on time horizon and risk tolerance (SEC Investor.gov: "the allocation that works best for you changes at different times in your life").
- Because assets earn different returns, the actual mix **drifts**. If equity rises faster than debt, equity becomes a larger share than planned and the portfolio carries more risk than intended.
- **Rebalancing** is bringing the mix back toward the target. SEBI's Financial Education Booklet encourages investors to "periodically review your financial needs / goals and review the portfolio".
- Common approaches (general educational descriptions):
  - **Calendar-based:** review on a schedule, such as once a year.
  - **Threshold-based:** act when a class drifts beyond a set band, such as 5 percentage points.
  - **Using new money:** direct fresh contributions to the under-weight class, so nothing needs to be sold.
- **Costs and taxes:** selling may trigger transaction charges, exit loads on mutual funds, and capital-gains tax (see "Exit Loads and Lock-ins" and "Capital Gains Tax Basics in India"). Rebalancing too often can cost more than the risk it removes.
- Rebalancing controls **risk**, not returns: it does not promise better performance than leaving the mix alone.

## Worked Example

Hypothetical numbers, ignoring costs and tax:

- Start: ₹10,00,000 split 60% equity (₹6,00,000) and 40% debt (₹4,00,000).
- Equity rises 20%, debt is unchanged: equity ₹7,20,000, debt ₹4,00,000, total ₹11,20,000.
- New mix: equity 64.3%, debt 35.7%. The portfolio now holds more equity risk than the 60% target.
- To return to 60/40 of ₹11,20,000, equity should be ₹6,72,000 and debt ₹4,48,000. That means moving **₹48,000** from equity to debt, or adding the shortfall to debt with new money instead.

This example describes the mechanics. It says nothing about whether equity will keep rising or fall next.

## Misconceptions

- "Rebalancing boosts returns." Its main job is keeping risk near the intended level.
- "You must rebalance at fixed intervals." Schedules and bands are conventions; the cost of trading and tax can outweigh a small drift.
- "Rebalancing means selling winners because they are expensive." It is about weights, not about judging whether an asset is cheap or dear.
