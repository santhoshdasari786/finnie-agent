# Volatility vs. Permanent Loss

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain that a price *falling* is not the same as capital being *lost for good*, describe what makes a loss permanent, and say why this distinction matters without implying that any fall will always recover.

## Core Concepts

- **Volatility** is how much and how quickly an investment's price moves. SEBI's Financial Education Booklet defines **volatility risk** as "the risk when the stock prices of a company may fluctuate, even when those companies aren't in danger of failing".
- **Permanent loss of capital** happens when the money cannot be expected to come back: a company stops operating, a borrower defaults and the debt is not repaid, an investment turns out to be fraudulent, or a price is paid so high that the asset never earns it back.
- SEBI's booklet separates related risks: **market (systematic) risk** from factors affecting the whole market, **unsystematic risk** specific to a company or industry (a new competitor, a regulatory change, a product recall), and **business risk**: "the risk that a business might stop its operations due to any unfavourable market or financial situation".
- A diversified, long-horizon investor mostly lives with volatility; a concentrated or leveraged investor is more exposed to permanent loss. SEBI notes volatility risk "can be managed by investing through Systematic Investment Plan of Mutual Funds or buying equities directly from the market in smaller lots over a period of time".
- A **correction** is described by SEBI's investor site as a temporary reversal of trend, while a **bear market** is a longer phase of falling prices (see "Market Corrections, Bear Markets and Recovery").
- Whether a fall is temporary depends on *why* it happened and *what the investor holds*: a broad market falling is a different situation from a single company failing.
- Volatility becomes a real loss when the investor must **sell at the low** (to meet an urgent need, or because of panic or a margin call). This is why time horizon and emergency savings matter.

## Worked Example

Two hypothetical investors each put ₹1,00,000 into a different investment. Illustration only:

| | Investor X | Investor Y |
|---|---|---|
| Holding | A diversified basket of many companies | One small company |
| What happens | Prices fall 30% in a market-wide decline, then regain much of it over the following years | The company loses its main customer, stops trading and its shares become worthless |
| Value at the low | ₹70,000 on paper | ₹0 |
| If X waits and the market recovers | The paper loss shrinks or disappears | Nothing to wait for |
| If X must sell at the low to pay a bill | The loss becomes real | |

The 30% fall was volatility for Investor X. For Investor Y the loss was permanent. X could also turn the fall into a real loss by being forced to sell at the bottom.

## Misconceptions

- "A falling price means the money is gone." Not necessarily: a fall on paper becomes a loss only if the investor sells, or if the underlying business or borrower cannot recover.
- "Markets always recover, so no loss is permanent." Markets as a whole have recovered from past falls, but individual companies, bonds and schemes can fail permanently, and no source can promise a recovery.
- "Low volatility means low risk." A steady-looking investment can still carry credit, fraud or inflation risk. SEBI's booklet lists seven distinct risks.
