# Pump-and-Dump Schemes and Stock Tip Fraud

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain how a pump-and-dump scheme works, show the loss with an example, list its warning signs, and point to official channels, without telling the user which stock is or is not a scheme.

## Core Concepts

- A **pump and dump** has two parts (Investor.gov). In the **pump**, promoters try to boost the price of a stock with false or misleading statements about the company. In the **dump**, the fraudsters sell their own holdings into the market once the price has been pumped up.
- The schemes often happen on the internet, where it is common to see messages urging readers to buy a stock quickly. Promoters often claim to have "inside" information about a development that will be positive for the stock.
- After the fraudsters sell and stop promoting, **the price typically falls and investors lose their money** (Investor.gov).
- Targets are often **small, thinly traded stocks**, where a burst of buying can move the price a lot. FINRA describes three elements: the setup (choosing a target), the pump, and the dump.
- Today the "pump" is often delivered through **social media, messaging groups and videos**, which can make a tip look like friendly advice or popular opinion.
- **Warning signs:** pressure to act quickly, claims of secret or inside information, guaranteed or very large returns in a short time, a push to move to a private group, promoters whose registration cannot be confirmed, and heavy promotion of an obscure company.
- SEBI's educational material also lists pump-and-dump schemes among the frauds it warns against, together with unregistered advisers and finfluencers.
- Spreading false information to move a price is illegal, and **disseminating hot tips can itself be unlawful** (SEBI Financial Education Booklet, as quoted in the scams article). Investors who suspect it can report it.
- The people hurt most are those who buy **after** the price has already risen, because the promoters are selling to them.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical thinly traded stock is at ₹10. Promoters, who already hold shares bought at ₹10, post that "a big contract is coming, buy before Friday".

- The price rises to ₹25 as people buy.
- A follower with ₹50,000 buys 2,000 shares at ₹25.
- The promoters sell their shares around ₹25 and stop posting. Buying dries up and the price falls to ₹8.
- The follower's holding is worth 2,000 × ₹8 = ₹16,000, a loss of ₹34,000, while the promoters sold at ₹25 and gained.

The numbers are invented; they show how the structure moves money from late buyers to early sellers.

## Misconceptions

- "If many people are buying, it must be real." Rising prices and crowds are exactly what the scheme manufactures.
- "A tip that proves right once is reliable." A promoter can get one price rise right by causing it.
- "Only unsophisticated investors are caught." Convincing messages and screenshots persuade many people.
- "I can get out before the dump." The sellers set the timing; those who join late rarely can.
