# Loss Aversion and Panic Selling

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain loss aversion, describe how it can turn a temporary fall into a realised loss, and outline preparation habits, without telling a user to hold or sell any holding.

## Core Concepts

- **Loss aversion** is the tendency to feel the pain of a loss more strongly than the pleasure of an equal gain. The idea comes from behavioural research (prospect theory). The SEC's behavioural bulletin links related tendencies to the **disposition effect**, "to hold on to losing investments too long and sell winning investments too soon".
- **Panic selling** is selling because of fear during a sharp fall, often near the low. The SEC bulletin describes the contraction after a bubble as a "panic" involving "wide-scale selling".
- Loss aversion pulls in two opposite directions: some investors **sell everything to stop the pain**, while others **refuse to sell a loser** to avoid admitting the loss. Both are driven by feeling, not by the investment's prospects.
- A **fall on paper becomes a real loss only when it is realised**, by selling, or when the underlying asset cannot recover (see "Volatility vs. Permanent Loss"). Selling in a panic converts a temporary fall into a permanent one.
- **Why recovery needs patience:** after a loss, a larger percentage gain is needed to get back to even (see "Market Corrections, Bear Markets and Recovery").
- **Preparation helps** (general educational knowledge): keeping emergency savings so investments need not be sold at a bad time; choosing a mix of assets whose swings you can live with; deciding in advance what would make you change course; and checking investments less often.
- **Automatic investing** can help. AMFI says a SIP "does not guarantee profits or prevent losses" but "encourages regular investing and helps you avoid emotional decisions during market ups and downs".
- **Questions to ask before selling in a fall:** Has my goal or time horizon changed? Has the business or fund changed, or only the price? Do I need this money soon?
- Whether to sell is a personal decision; this article explains the behaviour, not the choice.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical investor holds ₹10,00,000 in a diversified equity fund for a goal 15 years away. Illustration only:

- The market falls and the holding drops 25%, to ₹7,50,000. The investor feels the loss strongly and sells.
- If the market later recovers, the investor who sold missed the recovery and has locked in the ₹2,50,000 loss.
- A second investor with the same holding, an emergency fund and a plan to review only once a year, does not sell. Whether the holding recovers is not guaranteed, but this investor keeps the chance.
- A third investor, whose goal was only 18 months away, has a different problem: the horizon was too short for that holding. This shows that the answer depends on goals and time horizon, not only on feelings.

The example shows how preparation changes behaviour. It does not predict that markets recover.

## Misconceptions

- "Selling after a fall always protects you." If the asset later recovers, selling at the low locks in the loss.
- "Holding after a fall always works out." Not every investment recovers; some losses are permanent.
- "Only weak investors panic." Most people feel loss aversion; planning is what reduces its effect.
- "Checking daily keeps me safe." Frequent checking tends to increase stress and impulsive decisions.
