# Benchmarks and Market Indices

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what an index is, how it differs from a fund, what a benchmark is for, and the pitfalls of comparing a fund or portfolio with an index, without naming any index as "the right" yardstick for a specific user.

## Core Concepts

- A **market index** is a number that summarises the price movement of a chosen group of securities, so people can see how a market or segment is doing as a whole. India's widely quoted examples are the **Sensex** (BSE) and the **Nifty** (NSE).
- An index is **not an investment** by itself; you cannot buy "the Nifty" directly. You can buy a fund that tries to copy it. SEBI's Beginner's Guide: an index fund "replicates the performance of a broad market index such as the SENSEX or NIFTY", and BSE's investor booklet describes ETFs as funds "that track indices such as Sensex, Nifty".
- A **benchmark** is the index (or mix of indices) against which a fund's performance is judged. AMFI's booklet says active funds aim "to outperform a benchmark index", while passive funds aim "to mirror the performance of a particular market index".
- The benchmark should **match the fund's job**: a large-company equity fund should be compared with a large-company index, not a small-company or debt index. A mismatch makes the fund look better or worse than it really is.
- Index levels are shown as **price indices** (price changes only) or **total return indices** (price changes plus reinvested dividends). A fair comparison with a fund's returns uses the total return version, because the fund's returns include dividends (general educational knowledge; see "Review Metadata").
- Indices are **rule-based and change over time**: companies enter and leave as the rules require, so an index's history is not the history of a fixed set of companies.
- A fund beating or trailing its benchmark in one period says little about the next; compare over comparable periods, after costs (see "Comparing Investment Products Fairly").

## Worked Example

A hypothetical large-company equity fund and its benchmark over one year. Illustrative numbers only:

- The benchmark index (total return version) rose 12%.
- The fund rose 10%, after its expense ratio was deducted.
- The fund *trailed* its benchmark by 2 percentage points.

If the same fund had been compared with a *small-company* index that rose 25%, it would look much worse, and against a *bond* index that rose 6% it would look much better. Neither comparison would be fair, because those indices describe different assets. Picking the benchmark decides the verdict.

## Misconceptions

- "The index return is what an index-fund investor receives." A fund tracking an index will usually earn slightly less, because of its costs and small tracking differences.
- "Any index will do as a benchmark." The benchmark must resemble the fund's holdings and mandate.
- "An index is a list of the best companies." It is a set of companies selected by published rules, mainly size and trading activity, not a quality ranking.
- "Beating the index last year means the fund will again." Past outperformance does not predict future results (see "Past Performance and Survivorship Bias").
