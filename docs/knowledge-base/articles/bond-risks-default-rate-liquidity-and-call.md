# Bond Risks: Default, Interest Rate, Inflation, Liquidity and Call Risk

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to name and explain the main risks of a bond, explain why holding to maturity does not remove all of them, and show how a call or a rate rise can change the outcome, without recommending bonds.

## Core Concepts

- A **bond** is a debt security: the investor lends money to a government or company for a period at a stated **coupon**, and expects the **principal** back at maturity (SEBI, Investor.gov). The coupon is fixed, but the **price of the bond can fluctuate**, so total return includes coupon income and any gain or loss on price.
- **Default (credit) risk:** the issuer may not pay interest or principal on time because of changes in the economy or its own finances. Listed bonds carry **credit ratings**; higher ratings suggest lower risk and lower yields, lower ratings higher risk and higher yields.
- **Ratings are one opinion.** SEBI's page says not to rely solely on credit ratings: they come from a rating agency, can change quickly, and should be read together with the issuer's repayment history, profitability and credit metrics.
- **Interest rate risk:** when market interest rates rise, the price of existing bonds may fall, and the reverse. A bond sold before maturity can fetch more or less than face value. Investor.gov explains that new bonds then pay more, so an older bond with a lower rate may have to be sold at a discount.
- **Inflation risk:** a fixed coupon buys less if prices rise (Investor.gov).
- **Liquidity risk:** if few buyers exist when you want to sell, you may have to sell at a loss, or not be able to sell at all.
- **Call risk:** some bonds can be bought back by the issuer **before maturity**, which an issuer may do if interest rates decline, much like a homeowner refinancing a mortgage. The investor then gets the principal back early and may be unable to reinvest at the same yield.
- **Holding to maturity** removes the price effect of interest rate changes if the issuer pays in full, but not default, inflation or call risk. Early exit exposes the investor to the market price.
- The longer a bond's maturity and the lower its rating, the more these risks tend to matter; see the interest-rate and credit articles for the mechanics.
- Bonds sold by unregistered sellers are a warning sign (Investor.gov), so check that an offer is regulated.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical bond with a face value of ₹1,000 pays a 9% coupon (₹90 a year) and is callable. All figures are invented.

- **Call:** market rates fall, so the issuer calls the bond at ₹1,000 and the investor, who wanted ₹90 a year, can now reinvest only at a lower coupon, say 7%, getting ₹70 a year on ₹1,000: ₹20 less each year than before.
- **Rate rise:** if instead rates rise and a similar new bond pays 11%, an investor who must sell the 9% bond early would have to offer a price below ₹1,000 to compete.
- **Default:** if the issuer fails to pay, the investor may receive less than the principal, whatever the coupon was.

The numbers are invented to show direction, not size.

## Misconceptions

- "Bonds are safe because the coupon is fixed." The coupon is fixed; the price, the issuer's ability to pay, and real value are not.
- "A high rating means no risk." Ratings are opinions and can change quickly.
- "If I hold to maturity nothing can go wrong." Default, inflation and call risk can still apply.
- "A callable bond always runs to maturity." The issuer may repay it early.
