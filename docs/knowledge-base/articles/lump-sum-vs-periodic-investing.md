# Lump-Sum vs Periodic Investing and Rupee-Cost Averaging

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain the difference between investing a sum all at once and investing the same total in equal parts over time, show with numbers how each can come out ahead, and explain that neither is guaranteed to do better.

## Core Concepts

- **Lump-sum investing** puts the whole amount in at one time.
- **Periodic investing** puts in equal amounts at regular intervals, such as a SIP. Investor.gov calls this **dollar-cost averaging**, and in India it is often called **rupee-cost averaging**: investing equal portions at regular intervals regardless of the ups and downs in the market.
- Because the amount is fixed, an investor buys **more units when the price is low and fewer when it is high** (Investor.gov). The result is an average cost per unit that is lower than the simple average of the prices paid, when prices rise and fall.
- Investor.gov says this approach can help manage risk by following a consistent pattern of adding new money over a long period. It manages the risk of putting everything in at a bad moment; it does not remove market risk.
- **When prices mostly rise**, money that was invested earlier has more time to grow, so a lump sum tends to finish ahead of money added gradually. **When prices mostly fall**, the gradual investor has bought some units more cheaply and tends to finish ahead.
- Nobody knows in advance which pattern will occur, which is why neither approach is guaranteed to be better.
- The practical difference is often about the source of money: salary arrives monthly, so periodic investing is how many people invest, while a lump sum exists only when there is a large amount available.
- Periodic investing also supports a habit and reduces the temptation to time the market.
- Costs such as exit loads and taxes apply to each purchase separately, so the details of a product matter.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical investor has ₹40,000 and buys units of a fund. All prices are invented.

**Scenario 1: bumpy but ending where it began.** Monthly unit prices are ₹100, ₹80, ₹125 and ₹100.
- Periodic (₹10,000 each month): units bought = 100 + 125 + 80 + 100 = 405. Average cost per unit = ₹40,000 / 405 = ₹98.77, below the simple average price of ₹101.25. Value at ₹100 = ₹40,500.
- Lump sum (₹40,000 at ₹100): 400 units, value at ₹100 = ₹40,000.
- Periodic ends ₹500 ahead.

**Scenario 2: steady rise.** Prices are ₹100, ₹110, ₹120 and ₹130.
- Periodic: 351.17 units, value at ₹130 = ₹45,651.
- Lump sum: 400 units, value at ₹130 = ₹52,000. Lump sum ends ₹6,349 ahead.

**Scenario 3: steady fall.** Prices are ₹100, ₹90, ₹80 and ₹70.
- Periodic: 478.97 units, value at ₹70 = ₹33,528.
- Lump sum: 400 units, value at ₹70 = ₹28,000. Periodic ends ₹5,528 ahead, but both investors lost money.

Charges, taxes and the time value of money on uninvested cash are ignored. These are illustrations, not forecasts.

## Misconceptions

- "Periodic investing guarantees a profit or protects against loss." It lowers the average cost in a bumpy market, but in a falling market it still loses money, as Scenario 3 shows.
- "Rupee-cost averaging always beats a lump sum." In a rising market the lump sum did better in Scenario 2.
- "It is a way to beat the market." It is a way of spreading the timing; it does not change the investment's own returns.
- "The average cost is the average of the prices." It is the total invested divided by total units, which is lower than the simple average of the prices when prices vary.
