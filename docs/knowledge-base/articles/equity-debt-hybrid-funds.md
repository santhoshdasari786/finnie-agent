# Equity, Debt and Hybrid Mutual Funds

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain the main mutual fund scheme categories and what each broadly aims to do, and explain that SEBI's category rules and thresholds are revised from time to time, so the current rules must be confirmed before they are quoted.

## Critical Constraint

SEBI sets the scheme categories and their minimum-allocation rules in its Master Circular for Mutual Funds and revises them. The **numbers in the illustration table below are as printed in AMFI's booklet (which cites the circular dated 20 March 2026)**. Treat them as a snapshot, and confirm against the current circular or the scheme's own document before quoting any of them as current.

## Core Concepts

- A **mutual fund** pools money from many investors and invests it according to the scheme's stated objective. SEBI's Financial Education Booklet groups schemes into **equity, debt, hybrid, solution-oriented and other** schemes.
- **Equity schemes** invest mainly in equities and equity-related instruments and are described by AMFI as "suitable for long-term wealth creation, though it may be volatile in the short term". They are sub-divided by company size (large, mid, small), by style (value, contra, dividend yield), by breadth (flexi cap, multi cap, focused) and by theme or sector.
- **ELSS** (Equity Linked Savings Scheme) is an equity scheme with a 3-year lock-in that has been eligible for a deduction under the old tax regime (AMFI booklet).
- **Debt schemes** "primarily invest in bonds and fixed-income securities, aiming to provide income and capital preservation" (AMFI). They are sub-divided mainly by the length (maturity or duration) of what they hold, from overnight and liquid funds to long-duration and gilt funds, and by credit quality. AMFI warns income "is not guaranteed".
- **Hybrid schemes** "invest in a mix of equities and debt securities", and SEBI has classified them into several sub-categories that differ in how much they hold in each.
- **Solution-oriented schemes** (retirement funds and children's funds) have a lock-in of at least five years or until a milestone.
- **Passive funds** (index funds and ETFs) copy an index; **active funds** try to beat a benchmark (see "Index Funds vs. Actively Managed Funds").
- A category name tells you the **rules the fund must follow**, not how risky or how good a specific fund is. Two funds in the same category can differ widely in holdings and risk. SEBI also requires a **risk-o-meter** label for each scheme.
- A fund's **plan** (direct or regular) and **option** (growth or IDCW) are separate choices from its category (see "Direct vs. Regular Plans").

## Worked Example

An illustration of how category rules read, as printed in AMFI's booklet. The figures can change:

| Category | Rule described by AMFI (March 2026 circular) |
|---|---|
| Large Cap Fund | At least 80% in equity of large-cap companies |
| Small Cap Fund | At least 65% in equity of small-cap companies |
| Liquid Fund | Debt and money market securities with maturity up to 91 days only |
| Gilt Fund | Minimum 80% in government securities across maturities |
| Aggressive Hybrid Fund | Between 65% and 80% in equity and equity-related instruments, the rest mainly debt |

A "large cap" label therefore means the fund keeps at least the stated share in large companies. The remaining share can be elsewhere, and the fund's day-to-day holdings can differ from another large-cap fund's.

## Misconceptions

- "Debt funds are safe like fixed deposits." Debt funds can lose value through interest-rate moves and credit events, and unlike bank deposits are not covered by deposit insurance.
- "Hybrid means low risk." The risk depends on the mix; an aggressive hybrid fund holds mostly equity.
- "Same category means same performance or risk." The category sets minimum rules only.
- "Liquid funds have no risk." They invest in short-term securities, which reduces but does not remove risk.
