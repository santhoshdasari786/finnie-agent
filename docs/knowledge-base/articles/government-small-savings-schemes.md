# Government Small Savings Schemes: Sukanya Samriddhi, KVP, NSC and PPF

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to describe what the main government savings schemes are and how they differ, and tell the user that rates, limits and tax treatment change and must be checked on official sources, without choosing a scheme for anyone.

## Core Concepts

- SEBI's booklet says the Government of India accepts deposits from the public, and some of these are also tax-saving instruments. Examples named: **National Savings Certificates (NSC), Kisan Vikas Patra (KVP), Post Office Savings Certificates, Sukanya Samriddhi (the booklet spells it "Samrudhi") and the Public Provident Fund (PPF)**. They have different investment durations and **specific interest rates**, which are announced by the government and are not quoted here.
- **Sukanya Samriddhi:** aims to promote the welfare of the girl child. A natural or legal guardian opens the account for a girl child (the booklet gives an age limit and a limit on the number of accounts per family). Premature closure is allowed only in specified situations such as the depositor's death or compassionate grounds, and withdrawals for higher education or marriage are allowed in part after a stated age.
- **Kisan Vikas Patra:** a small savings certificate from India Post designed to double the investment after a predetermined time, bought at a post office, with a minimum amount and no maximum.
- **National Savings Certificate:** an initiative to invest while saving on income tax, obtained from a post office; a joint account is allowed and an adult can open one for a minor.
- **Public Provident Fund:** a government savings scheme available through a post office or banks, with one account per citizen and a nomination facility; contributions may qualify for a tax deduction up to a limit, and loans against the balance are possible under conditions. See the PPF, EPF and NPS article.
- These are schemes **run by the Government of India** (the booklet's wording is that the government accepts these deposits), but they are **less liquid** than a bank savings account (lock-ins, restricted withdrawals) and the return is fixed by the scheme's rules, not by the market.
- **Inflation** can still reduce the real value of the return, and tax treatment of the interest differs by scheme.
- Each scheme has rules on who can invest, how much, how long, and what happens on early exit. Check the scheme's current notification, the post office or the bank.
- The booklet's descriptions are from around 2020; schemes and their terms have been revised since.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical person compares the **structure** of three options for money they will not need for several years. No rates are used, because they change.

| Feature | Savings bank account | A fixed-term savings certificate | A guardian-opened girl-child account |
|---|---|---|---|
| Access to money | any time | locked or restricted until maturity | restricted, partial use after a stated age |
| Return | variable | fixed by the scheme | fixed by the scheme |
| Typical use | day-to-day needs | medium to long goals | one specific long-term purpose |

The comparison shows what to check: liquidity, how the return is set, who may open it, and the exit rules. It makes no recommendation.

## Misconceptions

- "Government schemes have the same rate for ever." Rates are announced by the government and revised.
- "They are as liquid as a bank account." Lock-ins and withdrawal limits apply.
- "Tax benefits are the same across schemes." Each scheme has its own tax treatment; check the current rules.
- "Government-backed means inflation-proof." The return can still fall behind price rises.
