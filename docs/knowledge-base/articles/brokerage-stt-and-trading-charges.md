# Brokerage, STT and Other Trading Charges

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to list the kinds of costs involved in trading shares, explain where to see them, show how costs affect the break-even point of a trade, and tell the user to take current rates from the broker's published charges and official notices, never from memory.

## Critical Constraint

**Rates and slabs of these charges differ between brokers and are changed by the Government, SEBI and the exchanges.** This article names the *types* of charges and never states a rate as current. For any figure, point the user to the broker's published charge schedule and the latest official notice.

## Core Concepts

- **Brokerage** is what the broker charges for executing a trade. Brokers differ in how they charge (a percentage of trade value, a flat fee per order, or a plan). SEBI's Financial Education Booklet advises: "carefully note all the charges/fees/brokerage that are applicable on your accounts and keep a record of the same" and "don't pay more than the agreed brokerage/charges".
- **Securities Transaction Tax (STT)** is a tax levied on certain securities transactions on exchanges. Whether it applies, and at what rate, depends on the type of trade.
- **Exchange transaction charges** are fees charged by the stock exchange on traded value.
- **Regulatory and statutory charges** commonly include a SEBI turnover fee, stamp duty on purchases, and GST on brokerage and certain other charges.
- **Depository charges:** the demat account may carry an annual maintenance charge and a per-transaction charge when shares are sold from it. SEBI's booklet describes the Basic Services Demat Account for small holdings, with nil or low maintenance charges (the booklet's slabs are dated and not quoted here).
- **Impact cost and spread:** the difference between the buying and selling price at a given moment also costs money, though it does not appear as a line item.
- **Where to see them:** the **contract note** the broker issues for each trade "contains details of the transaction such as securities bought/sold, traded price, time of trade, brokerage, etc." (SEBI booklet).
- Charges apply **on both buying and selling**, so a round trip pays them twice. They are paid whether or not the trade makes a profit.
- **Capital-gains tax** is separate from the trading charges above and is paid on profits (see "Capital Gains Tax Basics in India").

## Worked Example

A made-up illustration of break-even, with an **assumed** total of all charges, not real rates:

- An investor buys 100 shares at ₹500: ₹50,000 invested.
- Suppose all charges on the purchase total ₹120 and on the later sale another ₹120, an assumed ₹240 over the round trip.
- To merely recover those charges, the price must rise by ₹240 ÷ 100 shares = **₹2.40 per share**, about 0.5% above the purchase price.
- If the investor sells at ₹500 again, the result is a loss of ₹240, even though the share price did not move.

The more often someone trades, the more often this break-even has to be cleared.

## Misconceptions

- "Zero brokerage means zero cost." Other charges, taxes and the spread still apply.
- "Charges only matter for frequent traders." Every trade pays them; long-term investors pay less often, not never.
- "The contract note is just a receipt." It is a legal record that shows each charge; checking it is how errors and overcharging are spotted.
- "All brokers charge the same." Fee structures differ widely; compare the full set of charges, not one headline number.
