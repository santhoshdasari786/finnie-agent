# Inflation and Purchasing Power

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain why rising prices erode the value of money over time, distinguish "more rupees" from "more purchasing power," and run a simple future-value-under-inflation calculation without improvising the math.

## Core Concepts

- **Inflation** is the general rise in prices over time, which means a fixed amount of money buys less in the future than it does today.
- **Purchasing power** is what actually matters to an investor or saver — not the number printed on a bank statement, but what that number can buy.
- Money sitting idle (e.g. in a non-interest-bearing account) *loses* purchasing power every year inflation is positive, even though the number itself doesn't shrink.
- Inflation is typically reported as an annualized percentage by a country's central bank or statistics agency, and it varies year to year — it is not a fixed constant.
- The relationship between growth rate and inflation rate determines whether an investment is actually building wealth in real terms (see "Nominal Returns vs. Real Returns").

## Worked Example

If today's assumed inflation rate is **4% per year** (illustrative, not a current RBI figure), ₹1,00,000 today would need to grow to roughly:

| Years from now | Equivalent purchasing power needed |
|---|---|
| 5 | ≈ ₹1,21,665 |
| 10 | ≈ ₹1,48,024 |
| 20 | ≈ ₹2,19,112 |

In other words, if an investment doesn't grow faster than inflation, its *buying power* shrinks even while the number on the statement grows.

*(Assumption: constant 4% annual inflation for illustration only — actual inflation varies year to year and must be checked against current central bank/statistical data, not assumed.)*

## Misconceptions

- "My balance went up" does not automatically mean "I'm wealthier" — compare the growth rate to the inflation rate over the same period.
- Inflation is not the same everywhere or every year — it varies by country and by time period, and historical averages are not guarantees of future inflation.
- Cash and very-low-yield instruments are not "risk-free" in real terms — they can carry meaningful *purchasing-power risk* even with zero nominal volatility.
