# Why Past Performance Does Not Guarantee Future Returns

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain why past returns do not predict future returns, describe survivorship bias and period-selection effects, and list what to examine alongside past returns, without ranking any fund or recommending one.

## Core Concepts

- **Past returns describe what happened under past conditions**: interest rates, valuations, the economy and the fund manager's choices. Those conditions change, so the same fund can do very differently next time.
- The SEC's bulletin on investor behaviour lists "focusing on past performance of mutual funds and ignoring fees" among behaviours that undermine returns: some investors focus on past annualised returns and tend to disregard the fund's expense ratios, transaction costs and loads.
- Mutual fund advertisements in India carry a standard statement that past performance may or may not be sustained in future (a widely seen disclosure; its exact wording was not read in this pass).
- **Survivorship bias:** performance tables often include only funds that still exist. Funds that did badly may have been merged or closed, which removes them from the record and makes the average of the survivors look better than what an investor in the whole group would have experienced.
- **Period-selection bias:** a return depends heavily on the start and end dates. A fund can look excellent over a window that begins at a market low and ordinary over a window that begins at a high.
- **Short periods mislead.** One strong year can come from luck or a single bet; consistency over comparable, longer periods tells more, although still not what comes next.
- **Look at more than the return:** the **risk** taken to get it (how far the fund fell in bad periods), the **costs** (see "Expense Ratios and Fees"), the **benchmark** it is compared with (see "Benchmarks and Market Indices"), and whether the **manager or strategy has changed**.
- **Comparing like with like:** use the same periods, after costs, against the right benchmark (see "How to Compare Two Investment Products Fairly").
- AMFI's SIP illustration carries a reminder: "the calculations shown are for informational and illustrative purposes only". Illustrations are not forecasts.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical group of 100 funds, with invented numbers:

- Five years ago there were 100 funds in a category. Over five years, 20 of the weakest were merged into others or closed.
- A table of "five-year returns" now lists the 80 survivors, and their average return looks good.
- An investor who had picked at random five years ago would have had a 1-in-5 chance of holding one of the closed funds, so the survivors' average overstates the typical experience.

Period selection: the same fund returns 18% a year from a market low to today, but 8% a year if measured from a market high. Neither number alone predicts the next period.

## Misconceptions

- "A top-ranked fund will stay top-ranked." Rankings change and leaders often do not repeat.
- "A long record is proof of skill." Long records can reflect favourable conditions or survivorship.
- "Returns are all that matters." Risk, cost and fit with the goal matter too.
- "A recent poor return means a fund is bad." A short, weak period may be ordinary variation; judge over suitable periods and against the right benchmark.
