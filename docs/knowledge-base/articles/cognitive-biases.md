# Common Cognitive Biases in Investing

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to name several common investing biases, explain each in a sentence with an example, and describe practical habits that reduce their effect, without labelling any user as biased or telling them what to buy or sell.

## Core Concepts

- A **cognitive bias** is a systematic mental shortcut that can lead to predictable mistakes. The SEC's Investor Bulletin summarising a Library of Congress report on U.S. investor behaviour names nine investing behaviours that can undermine performance; several are below.
- **Disposition effect:** "the tendency of an investor to hold on to losing investments too long and sell winning investments too soon". The report notes winners sold early often continue to outperform the losers still held.
- **Familiarity bias:** favouring investments from one's own country, region or company, and "glamour investments" that are well known and popular. It "may cause an investor's portfolio to be inadequately diversified, which can increase the portfolio's risk exposure".
- **Focusing on past performance and ignoring fees:** some investors look mainly at a fund's past annual returns and disregard its expense ratio, transaction costs and loads, despite the harm those costs do to returns.
- **Active trading:** regular buying and selling; the report concludes it "generally results in the underperformance of an investor's portfolio".
- **Manias and panics:** a rapid price rise driven by collective enthusiasm followed by wide-scale selling (see "FOMO and Herd Behaviour").
- **Other common biases** (general educational knowledge): *overconfidence* (overestimating one's knowledge or skill), *confirmation bias* (seeking information that agrees with what you already believe) and *anchoring* (fixing on a reference number, such as the price you paid or a past peak).
- BSE's investor booklet gives a plain-language rule: "Don't let greed overcome rational investment decisions. Don't get carried away by luring advertisements or market rumours."
- **Habits that help** (general educational knowledge): writing down the reason for an investment before making it, deciding in advance when you would sell, reviewing a portfolio on a schedule rather than daily, and using automatic investing such as SIPs, which AMFI says "helps you avoid emotional decisions during market ups and downs".

## Worked Example

A hypothetical investor holds two shares bought at ₹100 each. One rises to ₹140; the other falls to ₹70. Illustration of two biases:

- **Disposition effect:** the investor sells the one at ₹140 "to lock in the gain" and keeps the one at ₹70 "until it gets back to ₹100". The decision is driven by the purchase price, which is an anchor, not by what each company's prospects now look like.
- **A neutral question** would be: "If I had cash today, would I buy each of these at its current price, given what I know?" The answer may or may not match the earlier choice. The point is to base the decision on future prospects, not on the price paid.

The example does not say which share to hold; it shows how the question changes when the anchor is removed.

## Misconceptions

- "Only beginners have biases." Experienced investors show them too; they are common to most people.
- "Knowing about a bias removes it." Awareness helps, but rules and routines set in advance help more.
- "Bias means being irrational all the time." It describes predictable tendencies in particular situations.
- "Following a confident expert avoids bias." Experts have biases and conflicts of interest as well.
