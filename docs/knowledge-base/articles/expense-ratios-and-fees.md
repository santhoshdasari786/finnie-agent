# Expense Ratios and the Impact of Fees

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what a mutual fund's expense ratio covers, why even small percentage differences in fees compound meaningfully over long holding periods, and avoid stating specific current expense-ratio caps as fact without verification.

## Core Concepts

- **Base Expense Ratio (BER)** — per AMFI's own investor education booklet, this is "the core recurring expenses associated with managing and operating a mutual fund scheme": investment/advisory fees, marketing/selling/distribution expenses (including any distributor commission), administrative and operating expenses, and scheme servicing costs.
- **Total Expense Ratio (TER)** — the complete cost of investing: `TER = Base Expense Ratio + Brokerage Cost + Transaction Cost (for executing trades) + Statutory levies (including GST)`. TER is the more complete number — BER alone understates total cost.
- Fees are **recurring**, not one-time — even a fraction of a percent of difference in TER compounds every single year the money stays invested, not just once.
- **Exit load** is separate from the expense ratio — it's a fee charged specifically when redeeming units, deducted from the NAV at redemption (e.g. NAV ₹100 with a 1% exit load → redemption price ₹99/unit).
- Regulatory bodies (in India, SEBI, via circulars implemented through AMFI) set rules around how expense ratios are structured and capped — these caps/structures differ by fund category and can change, so **always check the fund's current factsheet/SID for its actual current TER rather than assuming a figure.**
- Direct vs. Regular mutual fund plans carry different expense ratios because Regular plans include distributor commission embedded in the fee, while Direct plans don't — see "Direct vs. Regular Mutual Fund Plans" for that distinction.
- Fees are one of the few things an investor has full, certain control over when selecting a product — unlike future returns, which are uncertain, the fee is known (or at least knowable) up front.

### A Confirmed, Current Example (Scoped — Not a Universal Cap)

AMFI's investor education booklet (referencing a SEBI Master Circular dated 20 March 2026) states that **index funds specifically** may charge a maximum base expense ratio of **0.90% of daily net assets**. This is cited here because it was directly verified against the primary document's actual text, not inferred — but it applies only to the index-fund category, not to mutual funds generally, and like any regulatory cap it can change in a future circular. Don't generalize this figure to other fund categories (active equity, debt, etc.) without checking their own applicable caps.

## Worked Example

Illustrative only — demonstrates the mechanism, not a specific real product's figures. ₹10,00,000 invested for 20 years, assumed 10% gross annual return before fees:

| Expense ratio | Approx. net annual return | Approx. ending value after 20 years |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2% | ≈ 9.8% | ≈ ₹64,60,000 |
| 1.0% | ≈ 9.0% | ≈ ₹56,00,000 |
| 2.0% | ≈ 8.0% | ≈ ₹46,60,000 |

An 0.8–1.8 percentage-point difference in annual fees compounds into a difference of several lakh rupees over 20 years on the same gross return assumption — this is the core reason fee comparison matters as much as return comparison.

*(The 10% gross return assumption and resulting figures are illustrative for demonstrating fee compounding, not a forecast or a real product's performance.)*

## Misconceptions

- A fee that sounds small (e.g. "just 1%") is deducted every year the money is invested, not once — its cumulative effect over a long horizon is much larger than the headline number suggests.
- Expense ratio is not the *only* cost — exit loads, brokerage (for ETFs), and transaction costs also reduce net returns (see "How to Compare Two Investment Products Fairly").
- A lower expense ratio does not automatically mean a "better" fund if the strategies/risk profiles being compared are different — compare like-for-like objectives first, then compare cost among similar options.
