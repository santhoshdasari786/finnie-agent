# KYC for Investors: What It Is and Why It Is Required

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what KYC is, why it is required, and the common ways it is completed, while telling the user that documents, forms and procedures change and must be confirmed with the institution or SEBI.

## Core Concepts

- **KYC** stands for Know Your Customer. SEBI's investor site calls it a mandatory process implemented by financial institutions, including banks, mutual fund companies and stockbrokers, to verify the identity and address of customers.
- **Why it exists:** to prevent fraud, identity theft and money laundering, to protect investors' accounts from unauthorised use, and to comply with anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing rules. SEBI's page says it is a requirement under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002.
- **What it involves:** proof of identity (for example PAN or Aadhaar) and proof of address, and in-person verification (IPV), which many institutions now complete digitally through a video call or Aadhaar-based e-KYC (SEBI investor site).
- **e-KYC:** Aadhaar-based electronic verification allows quick online completion without paper.
- **Video KYC:** verification through a video call.
- **Centralised KYC (CKYC):** once registered, KYC details are stored in a central database that participating institutions can use, so the same checks need not be repeated.
- **Effect on the investor:** without completed KYC, access to products such as mutual funds and stock trading, and to loans and cards, is restricted; verified details also help an institution authenticate the investor and speed up complaint handling after unauthorised transactions or service problems.
- **Keeping details current:** documents, address and mobile number should be kept up to date with the institution, since the account's verification depends on them.
- **Safety:** KYC documents contain sensitive data. Share them only with verified, registered institutions through official channels, not with callers, links or chat contacts who offer to "complete your KYC" (see the account-protection article).

Because this area changes through SEBI circulars, confirm the current requirements and forms with the intermediary or SEBI before acting.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical person wants to start investing in mutual funds.

1. They choose a registered fund house, distributor or platform and check its registration.
2. They provide proof of identity and address, completing the in-person verification through video or Aadhaar-based e-KYC if offered.
3. After verification the KYC status is updated, the account is activated, and they can invest. If KYC records already exist in the central database, the process may be shorter.
4. Later they receive a message asking them to "update KYC within two hours or the account will be frozen" through a link. This does not match the process above, so they contact the institution through its official website or app instead of using the link.

The scenario is invented to show the sequence and a common scam pattern; it is not a legal description of the procedure.

## Misconceptions

- "KYC is a one-time formality I can ignore." It is a legal requirement that gates access to products, and details need to stay current.
- "Anyone who asks for my KYC documents must be official." Scammers pose as institutions; only share through verified channels.
- "KYC makes an investment safe." It verifies the customer's identity; it says nothing about the safety of any product.
- "Doing KYC with one institution is useless for others." Centralised KYC exists so that completed records can be reused by participating institutions.
