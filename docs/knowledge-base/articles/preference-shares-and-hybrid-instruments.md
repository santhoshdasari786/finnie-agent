# Preference Shares and Other Hybrid Instruments

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what a hybrid instrument is, describe preference shares and their main variants, and show where each ranks for dividends and in a wind-up, without suggesting any instrument.

## Core Concepts

- SEBI's Beginner's Guide describes **hybrid instruments** as combining features of ownership (equity) and a loan (debt).
- **Preference shares** (preferred stock) entitle the holder to receive a **dividend at a fixed rate**. Importantly, this dividend must be paid **before any dividend can be paid to equity shareholders**.
- In the event of **liquidation**, a preference shareholder's claim on the company's surplus is **higher than that of equity holders but below the claims of creditors, bondholders and debenture holders** (SEBI guide).
- **Cumulative preference shares:** if a dividend is not paid in a year, it accumulates, and all arrears must be paid before any dividend is paid on equity shares.
- **Cumulative convertible preference shares:** the unpaid dividend accumulates, and after a specified date the shares convert into equity capital.
- **Participating preference shares:** the holder gets the fixed dividend and also a right to share in profits above it, linked to the dividend paid to equity shareholders above a specified level.
- Compared with ordinary shares, preference shares usually offer a **more predictable dividend and a higher ranking** but a more limited share in the company's growth; compared with bonds, the dividend is **not a legal obligation in the way interest is** and the claim ranks lower.
- A fixed dividend is only as good as the company's ability to pay: the ranking helps only if the company has the profit or assets to pay.
- Terms, such as whether dividends are cumulative or whether shares convert, are set by the issue's documents and must be read for each instrument.
- Tax treatment and trading arrangements were not covered by the source read.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical company is wound up and its remaining assets, after paying costs, are ₹100 crore, with these claims:

| Claimant | Amount owed | Order of payment |
|---|---|---|
| Bondholders and other creditors | ₹60 crore | first |
| Preference shareholders | ₹30 crore | second |
| Equity shareholders | residual | last |

Creditors receive ₹60 crore, preference shareholders ₹30 crore, and ₹10 crore is left for equity shareholders. If assets had been only ₹75 crore, creditors would be paid ₹60 crore, preference shareholders would get the remaining ₹15 crore (half of what they were owed), and equity shareholders would get nothing.

Another illustration: a cumulative preference share with a fixed annual dividend of ₹8 per share skips a year. The next year, ₹16 is owed to the preference shareholder before equity shareholders can receive any dividend.

The amounts are invented to show ranking and accumulation.

## Misconceptions

- "Preference shares are bonds." They are shares: the dividend is not a promise to pay in the same legal sense as interest, and the claim ranks below creditors.
- "A fixed dividend is guaranteed." It has priority over equity dividends but depends on the company's ability to pay.
- "Preference shareholders share fully in growth." Ordinary preference shares do not; only participating ones share in profits above the fixed dividend.
- "Cumulative means the dividend can never be lost." The arrears are owed but still need the company to be able to pay.
