# Direct vs. Regular Mutual Fund Plans

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain that direct and regular plans are two versions of the same scheme, show how a difference in cost builds up over years, and state what each route asks of the investor, without telling anyone which to pick.

## Core Concepts

- "A mutual fund scheme offers two plans: **Regular and Direct Plans**. Both are part of the same mutual fund scheme, have the same/common portfolio and are managed by the same fund manager" (AMFI booklet).
- **Regular plan:** the investor invests through an intermediary (such as a distributor or adviser). "Regular Plans involve distribution expenses/commission reflected in the applicable expense structure."
- **Direct plan:** invested in directly with the fund house or through a platform without a distributor. "Direct Plans do not involve distributor commission and generally have a lower expense ratio" (AMFI booklet).
- Each plan has its **own NAV**, because the expense ratios differ. The lower cost of a direct plan shows up as a higher NAV over time for the same underlying portfolio.
- **What changes for the investor:** AMFI says a direct plan is "suited for those who understand what kind of mutual funds are needed for different kinds of investment needs, are capable of researching these independently and able to identify/shortlist the funds to invest in, and then go through the process of actually investing without the help of an intermediary". A regular plan suits investors who prefer assistance from an intermediary.
- **What does not change:** the portfolio, the fund manager, the risk-o-meter label and the market risk are the same in both plans.
- The cost difference is **recurring**: it is taken from the fund's assets every year, so the gap widens through compounding (see "Expense Ratios and Fees").
- Paying for advice can be done in different ways (a commission inside the plan, or a separate fee to a registered adviser). Check that any intermediary is registered (see "How to Verify a Financial Intermediary").

## Worked Example

A hypothetical comparison. The expense ratios are assumed for illustration and are not any real scheme's figures:

- The scheme's portfolio earns **12% a year before costs** for both plans.
- Regular plan expense ratio **1.8%**, so net about 10.2%. Direct plan expense ratio **0.8%**, so net about 11.2%.
- ₹1,00,000 invested for 20 years:
  - Regular plan: about **₹6,97,600**.
  - Direct plan: about **₹8,35,800**.
- The one-percentage-point difference in cost leaves the direct plan about ₹1,38,000 ahead, on the same portfolio.

The example shows how cost compounds. It does not say that direct is better for any individual: a regular-plan investor may be getting advice, support or discipline that the cost pays for, and the example ignores taxes and exit loads.

## Misconceptions

- "Direct and regular plans hold different stocks." They hold the same portfolio.
- "Direct plans are riskier." The market risk is identical; the difference is cost and who assists the investor.
- "The commission is a one-time fee." In a regular plan it is built into the recurring expense ratio.
- "Advice is always worth the commission, or never worth it." It depends on what the investor receives and can do independently.
