# Bonus Issues, Rights Issues and Stock Splits

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what a bonus issue, rights issue and stock split are, show with a number how the share count and price change, and explain that these actions change how ownership is divided, not what the company is worth, without suggesting any action.

## Core Concepts

- **Bonus issue:** existing shareholders are issued additional shares in proportion to their holding, without paying anything (BSE booklet). A 1:1 bonus gives one new share for each share held.
- **Stock split:** an increase in the number of shares without a change in shareholders' equity. Companies often split to make shares more affordable. A split does not dilute existing shareholders' ownership, and dividends per share fall in proportion (Investor.gov). A two-for-one split turns 100 shares at $100 into 200 shares at $50.
- **Rights issue:** existing shareholders are offered the right to subscribe to newly issued shares in proportion to their existing holding (BSE booklet). It is an offer, not a gift: taking it means paying, and an investor can decline.
- In a bonus or split, the **number of shares rises and the price per share falls**, so the total value of the holding is roughly unchanged at the moment of the action. The market price afterwards moves with the market and the company's prospects, not because of the action itself.
- A rights issue **raises money** for the company and increases the share count; a shareholder who does not take it holds the same shares, but their percentage of the company falls.
- A **bonus issue** changes the share count and is not a dividend: it pays no cash. (How a company funds it is not covered by the sources read.)
- **Corporate action credits** such as dividends, bonus shares, splits and rights entitlements are processed through the depository system into the demat account (SEBI investor site).
- Where to look: the company's announcements on the exchanges, and SEBI's booklet points investors to such sources for corporate action information. Entitlement usually depends on dates set in the announcement (such as a record date), so check the announcement itself; the sources read here do not define these dates.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical investor holds 100 shares trading at ₹200, a holding worth ₹20,000.

- **1:1 bonus:** the investor receives 100 more shares, holding 200. The price adjusts to about ₹100, so the holding is still about ₹20,000.
- **2-for-1 split:** 100 shares become 200 at about ₹100; value about ₹20,000 as well.
- **Rights issue, 1 new share for every 5 held at ₹150:** the investor may buy 20 shares for ₹3,000. If taken, the holding is 120 shares and the money invested is ₹23,000 in total (the old ₹20,000 at market plus ₹3,000). A simple theoretical price afterwards is ₹23,000 ÷ 120 ≈ ₹191.67, below the earlier ₹200 because new shares were issued below the market price. If the investor declines, they still hold 100 shares, now a smaller share of a larger company.

Prices are illustrative; real market prices move with news and demand. The theoretical figure is simple arithmetic, not from a source.

## Misconceptions

- "A bonus issue or split makes me richer." The holding's value is roughly unchanged at the time; there are more shares at a lower price.
- "A lower price after a split means the stock got cheaper." The company is the same; each share is a smaller slice.
- "I must take up a rights issue." It is optional, but not taking it can reduce one's percentage ownership.
- "A bonus is the same as a dividend." A dividend pays cash; a bonus issues more shares.
