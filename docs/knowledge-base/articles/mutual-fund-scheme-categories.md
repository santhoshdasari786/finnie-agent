# Mutual Fund Scheme Categories in India (SEBI Classification)

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain that SEBI sorts mutual fund schemes into standard categories, describe the main groups and a few of their characteristics, and tell the user that the detailed rules were revised after the 2017 circular and must be checked in current documents.

## Core Concepts

- SEBI's circular of 6 October 2017 set out a **uniform classification** of mutual fund schemes, so that a category name means roughly the same thing across fund houses. It also gave each category a standard one-line description that a scheme must use.
- The main groups are **equity schemes, debt schemes, hybrid schemes, solution-oriented schemes** and **other schemes** (index funds and ETFs, and funds of funds).
- **Equity categories** in that circular include multi cap, large cap, large and mid cap, mid cap, small cap, dividend yield, value, contra, focused, sectoral or thematic, and ELSS (the tax-saving equity scheme with a statutory lock-in). A "large cap fund" is described as an open-ended equity scheme predominantly investing in large cap stocks; the size labels follow the market-capitalisation article.
- **Debt categories** are mostly separated by the maturity or duration of what they hold: overnight (securities maturing in one day), liquid (up to 91 days), ultra short, low, short, medium and long duration, money market, dynamic bond, corporate bond, banking and PSU, gilt (government securities) and floater funds.
- **Hybrid categories** mix equity and debt: conservative, balanced, aggressive, dynamic asset allocation or balanced advantage, multi asset allocation, arbitrage, and equity savings.
- **Solution-oriented** schemes are retirement funds and children's funds, which carry a lock-in.
- **Other schemes** are index funds and ETFs (which replicate an index) and funds of funds (which invest in other funds).
- The circular generally allowed a fund house only **one scheme per category**, with some stated exceptions.
- The category tells you the **kind** of assets and the broad rule on how much must be in them. It does not tell you the fund's quality, cost, or how its risk compares with another fund in the same category. The riskometer and the scheme documents carry that.
- The detailed minimum-allocation figures and several category definitions have been revised since 2017. This article does not quote them for that reason; check the current SEBI circulars and the scheme's own document.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical investor reads a scheme's document and finds the description: "An open ended debt scheme investing in government securities across maturity".

1. The description matches the **Gilt Fund** category in SEBI's classification (government securities across maturity).
2. That tells them what the scheme may hold, government securities, and that its price can still move with interest rates because it holds securities of varying maturity.
3. It does not tell them the scheme's expense ratio, past performance, or whether it suits them; those come from the factsheet and the scheme documents.

The scheme is invented and the example describes how to read a label, not a choice.

## Misconceptions

- "Same category means same risk or return." Funds in one category can hold different securities, cost different amounts and perform differently.
- "Debt fund means no risk." Debt categories carry interest-rate and credit risk, which depend on duration and what is held.
- "The 2017 rules are still exact." Later circulars changed some categories and limits; check the current text.
- "A fund house can name its scheme anything." Names and descriptions follow the categories and uniform descriptions.
