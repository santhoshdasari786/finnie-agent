# Understanding SEBI and India's Financial Regulators

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to name the main Indian financial regulators, say in one sentence what each oversees, and help a user work out which regulator is relevant to a given product, without suggesting that regulation removes investment risk.

## Core Concepts

- **SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India)** was "set up by an Act of Parliament of India in April 1992 with a mandate to protect the interest of investors, promote the development of and regulate the securities market" (SEBI Beginner's Guide). It supervises stock exchanges, brokers, depositories, mutual funds, investment advisers, credit rating agencies and listed companies' dealings with investors.
- SEBI's approach, per its guide, rests on four elements: **investor education, disclosure, safe market systems, and grievance redressal**. It registers "fit and proper" intermediaries, can inspect and investigate them, and can suspend or cancel registrations and impose penalties.
- **RBI (Reserve Bank of India)** is the central bank, established on 1 April 1935. It conducts monetary policy, supervises banks and other financial institutions, and is the banker to the Government of India (SEBI Financial Education Booklet). Commercial banks and non-banking finance companies are regulated by the RBI.
- **IRDAI** regulates insurance, including life insurance and unit-linked insurance plans. **PFRDA** regulates the National Pension System. The **Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA)** oversees deposits accepted by companies. The **National Housing Bank** oversees housing finance institutions. **State governments** oversee chit funds, co-operative society schemes and multi-level marketing schemes (SEBI booklet, Chapter 13).
- **Which regulator for what** (from the booklet's table): deposits mobilised by non-banking finance companies and Nidhi companies → RBI; gold saving schemes launched by jewellers → MCA and RBI; contracts of insurance and ULIPs → IRDAI; pension scheme under EPF or NPS → PFRDA (or IRDAI for insurance-type schemes); housing finance institutions → NHB; grievances against listed companies and intermediaries in the securities market → SEBI.
- **Regulation is not a guarantee.** A regulator sets rules, registers entities and enforces them; it does not guarantee returns or compensate for market losses. SEBI's own investor-protection arrangements are limited: for example, stock exchanges have funds that compensate investors when a broker is declared a defaulter, and depositories indemnify for their own or a participant's negligence.
- **Unregulated or unregistered** offers sit outside this protection and are a common source of fraud (see "Investor Scams and Unrealistic Returns").
- When a product mixes features (for example an insurance-linked investment), more than one regulator may be relevant.
- Names, scope and mandates can change through legislation; check the regulator's own website for the current position.

## Worked Example

A user asks where to complain in three hypothetical situations (see "Investor Grievance Channels" for the steps):

| Situation | Relevant regulator |
|---|---|
| A stock broker did not execute an order correctly | SEBI (and the exchange) |
| A bank refused to credit a failed transaction | RBI (bank complaint routes) |
| An insurer rejected a claim | IRDAI |
| An NPS account statement is wrong | PFRDA |

The first step in each case is usually to complain to the company or intermediary itself, then escalate to the regulator.

## Misconceptions

- "SEBI regulates every investment." It regulates the securities market. Banks sit under the RBI, insurance under IRDAI and pensions under PFRDA.
- "If a regulator oversees it, it cannot lose money." Regulated products still carry market, credit and other risks.
- "The regulator will recover my loss." Regulators enforce rules; recovery of money depends on the case and the law.
- "One regulator covers everything about a product." Some products touch more than one.
