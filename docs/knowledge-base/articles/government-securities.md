# Government Securities and Treasury Bills

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what a government security and a treasury bill are, why they are described as free from default risk, why their prices can still move, and how an individual can hold them, while pointing to the current RBI and exchange notices for operational details.

## Critical Constraint

Interest rates, auction details, product lists and investment limits for government securities change often and are set by the Government and the RBI. This article describes **how the instruments work**, not today's rates or limits. For any figure, point the user to the current RBI or Retail Direct notice.

## Core Concepts

- **Government securities (G-secs)** are instruments issued by the Government of India to raise money. They pay interest at a fixed rate on specific dates, typically half-yearly, and are available in a wide range of maturities, from short-dated (about one year) to long-dated (up to thirty years) (SEBI Beginner's Guide).
- G-secs are sovereign borrowing, so they are "free from risk of default (credit risk)" (same guide). SEBI's Financial Education Booklet likewise calls government bonds "effectively 'risk free'" because the government is trusted not to default.
- **Treasury bills (T-bills)** are short-term government instruments issued for cash management, at a **discount to face value**, with maturities from 14 to 365 days (SEBI Beginner's Guide). The investor's return is the difference between the discounted price paid and the face value received at maturity.
- **State Development Loans (SDLs)** are bonds issued by state governments (named in RBI's Retail Direct material; not covered in the SEBI documents).
- "No default risk" does **not** mean "no risk": G-sec prices move inversely with market interest rates, so a G-sec sold before maturity can fetch less than was paid (see "Bond Prices and Interest-Rate Risk"). Holding to maturity returns the face value and the promised interest.
- **How individuals can buy:** by subscribing through the RBI or buying on a stock exchange (SEBI Beginner's Guide). The **RBI Retail Direct** scheme, launched in November 2021, lets individuals open a gilt account directly with the RBI online and buy G-secs and T-bills in primary auctions and the secondary market (per RBI's FAQ and press release; verify current features).
- Government-linked investments also include savings bonds and Sovereign Gold Bonds, which have their own terms (see "Gold as an Investment").

## Worked Example

A T-bill example in the spirit of SEBI's guide, with hypothetical figures:

- A 91-day T-bill with a face value of ₹100 is sold at a discount for ₹98.50.
- At maturity the investor receives ₹100: a gain of ₹1.50 on ₹98.50 over 91 days.
- Annualising that gain gives roughly 6.1% a year (₹1.50 ÷ ₹98.50 × 365 ÷ 91). SEBI's guide quotes a similar illustrative T-bill at about 6.25% a year; real yields change with each auction.

There is no separate "interest payment": the return is built into the purchase discount.

## Misconceptions

- "Government securities have no risk at all." They carry no default risk, but they have interest-rate (price) risk and inflation risk.
- "A T-bill pays interest every few months." T-bills pay no periodic interest; they are bought at a discount and repaid at face value.
- "Only institutions can buy G-secs." Individuals can, through Retail Direct, exchanges or other channels.
- "The yield in an old article is today's yield." Auction yields change every time.
