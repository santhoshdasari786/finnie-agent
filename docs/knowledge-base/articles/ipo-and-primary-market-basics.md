# IPOs and the Primary Market: How a Public Issue Works

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what an IPO is, how book building discovers a price, how an investor applies and gets or does not get shares, and what documents to read, without saying whether any particular IPO is worth applying for.

## Core Concepts

- The **primary market** (new issues market) is where companies or governments raise money by issuing new securities. The **secondary market** is where already-issued securities are bought and sold on exchanges.
- An **initial public offer (IPO)** is the first offer of shares to the public by an unlisted company. A **follow-on public offer (FPO)** is a later public issue by a company that has already done an IPO.
- An IPO can be a **fresh issue** (new shares are issued, and the money goes to the company for the purpose stated) or an **offer for sale** (existing shareholders such as promoters sell their holding, and the money goes to them, not the company). Many offers combine both.
- Other ways of issuing include a **rights issue** (existing shareholders may subscribe to new shares in proportion to their holding) and a **preferential issue** (to an identified set of investors).
- **Offer documents:** a draft red herring prospectus is filed with SEBI with all issue details except the final price, and a red herring prospectus is issued before the issue opens. They describe the company's history, promoters, business, financials, risks, the purpose of the money and the terms of the issue.
- **Book building** is the process in which investors bid within a **price band** rather than at a fixed price. After bidding closes, the **cut-off price** is determined from demand. Retail investors can bid at the cut-off price and then pay the final discovered price.
- If an issue is **oversubscribed**, investors may receive fewer shares than they applied for, or none.
- **ASBA** (Application Supported by Blocked Amount), and UPI as a payment method within it, blocks the application money in the investor's own bank account. It is debited only on allotment and released if shares are not allotted.
- Allotted shares are credited to the investor's **demat account**, and the shares are then listed on a recognised exchange, where further trading takes place.
- **Timelines and rules change.** The BSE booklet describes listing in six working days after the issue closes, while SEBI's investor page on book building mentions three days. Verify the current listing timeline, price band rules and investor categories with SEBI or the exchange before relying on any of them.

## Worked Example

SEBI's investor site gives this illustration of book building (hypothetical): a company offers 5,000 shares in a price band of ₹120 to ₹144.

| Bid price | Shares demanded | Cumulative demand at or above the price |
|---|---|---|
| ₹120 | 500 | 6,500 |
| ₹130 | 1,000 | 6,000 |
| ₹135 | 2,500 | 5,000 |
| ₹140 | 1,500 | 2,500 |
| ₹144 | 1,000 | 1,000 |

The cut-off price is ₹135: it is the highest price at which the demand at or above it still covers all 5,000 shares. Investors who bid ₹135 or more are eligible for shares at ₹135, and bids below it get nothing. If total demand at that price is higher than the shares on offer, allotment may be partial, and the extra blocked money is released.

## Misconceptions

- "An IPO is a guaranteed gain on listing." The listing price can be above or below the issue price, and nothing is guaranteed.
- "Money goes to the company in every IPO." In an offer for sale, the money goes to the selling shareholders.
- "Applying means I will get shares." Oversubscribed issues allot fewer shares than applied for, or none.
- "Applying locks my money away." With ASBA the amount stays in the investor's own account, blocked until allotment, then released if no shares are allotted.
