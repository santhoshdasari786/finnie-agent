# Dividend Income and Taxation

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain that dividends are income and are taxed differently from capital gains, describe how tax deducted at source works, and send the user to current Income Tax Department guidance for any rate or threshold, without stating a figure as current law.

## Critical Constraint

**This article deliberately states no tax rates, thresholds or section numbers as current.** Dividend taxation in India has changed over time, and the Income-tax Act, 2025 is replacing the 1961 Act, so section numbers are changing as well. Any answer that needs a number must send the user to the Income Tax Department's current guidance or a qualified tax professional.

## Core Concepts

- A **dividend** is a share of a company's profit paid to shareholders (see "Dividends"). For the investor it is **income**, separate from any gain from selling the shares (see "Capital Gains Tax Basics in India").
- **Who pays the tax:** in India's current system, dividend income is generally taxed **in the hands of the shareholder** as part of total income, at the shareholder's applicable rate. In the past the company paid a distribution tax instead; that arrangement was changed. Confirm the current position at the Income Tax Department.
- **Tax deducted at source (TDS):** the Income Tax Department's pages on the dividend TDS provision show that a company paying dividends to a resident shareholder must deduct tax before paying, with a small annual threshold below which no deduction applies for individuals (archived versions of the section; the thresholds and rate differ across versions). TDS is not the final tax: it is credited against the shareholder's total tax liability.
- **Mutual fund payouts:** income distributed by a mutual fund (IDCW, formerly "dividend") is also treated as income for tax, and the fund's NAV falls by the amount paid out. Whether a fund pays out or reinvests is a scheme option.
- **Reporting:** dividend income should be reported in the income tax return even when TDS has been deducted, and the credit for TDS is claimed there. The investor's statements show the dividends received.
- **Differences from capital gains:** capital gains arise only when units or shares are *sold* at a profit; dividends arise when the company or fund *distributes* income, whether or not the investor sells.
- **Foreign dividends and special cases** follow different rules and should be checked separately.

## Worked Example

A purely hypothetical illustration of the mechanics, with an **assumed** tax rate, not a real one:

- A resident individual receives ₹20,000 in dividends during the year.
- Suppose the investor's income falls in a bracket taxed at an assumed 20%. Tax on the dividends would be ₹4,000, leaving ₹16,000 after tax.
- If the company had already deducted TDS of ₹2,000, the investor would show ₹4,000 tax on the dividends in the return, take credit for the ₹2,000 already deducted, and pay the remaining ₹2,000.

The percentage and the TDS amount are invented for illustration. The real rate and thresholds must come from the current rules.

## Misconceptions

- "Dividends are tax-free." They are income and generally taxable for the receiving investor.
- "TDS means the tax is fully settled." TDS is an advance deduction at a set rate; the investor's actual liability can be higher or lower.
- "Dividend tax and capital gains tax are the same thing." They apply to different events and are computed differently.
- "A reinvested dividend is not taxable." The dividend is income when received, even if the money is put back into the investment.
