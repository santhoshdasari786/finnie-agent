# Tax Planning, Tax Avoidance and Tax Evasion

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain the three terms, describe why the legal line is about substance and honesty, and tell users that questions about their own tax position belong with a qualified professional, while never suggesting ways to reduce tax unlawfully.

## Critical Constraint

Tax law, its section numbers and its rules on avoidance change, and the Income-tax Act, 2025 is replacing the 1961 Act. This article explains the **concepts**. For specific provisions, penalties and thresholds, point users to the Income Tax Department's current guidance or a qualified tax professional.

## Core Concepts

- **Tax planning** is arranging your finances to use the exemptions, deductions and incentives that the law itself provides, for example by choosing eligible savings products. It is lawful when the facts are reported honestly and the arrangement is what it appears to be. (General knowledge.)
- **Tax avoidance** is using the letter of the law to reduce tax in ways the law did not intend, often through arrangements entered mainly to gain a tax benefit. An Income Tax Department press release from 2013 states that tax mitigation is recognised while avoidance "is frowned upon" (seen via search summary).
- **The General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR)** lets tax authorities treat an arrangement as an *impermissible avoidance arrangement* where its main purpose is a tax benefit and it lacks commercial substance or is set up in an unusual way; they can then disregard or recharacterise it (via search summary of the department's material).
- **Tax evasion** is illegal: deliberately hiding income, misreporting, or falsifying records to pay less tax. The Income-tax Act treats a wilful attempt to evade tax as an offence with penalties and possible prosecution that scale with the amount involved, and separate penalties apply for false entries (via search summary of the department's pages).
- **The line** is not the amount of tax saved. Saving a lot of tax through an eligible, genuinely held product can be lawful; saving a little by hiding income is evasion.
- **Substance over form:** an arrangement that exists only on paper, or that misdescribes what really happened, is at risk even if each step looks legal.
- **Disclosure matters.** Reporting all income, keeping records and claiming only what you are entitled to keep a person on the right side of the line.
- **Tax questions about a specific person** (which deduction applies, how a gain is taxed) depend on their facts and the current law, and are for a qualified professional.

## Worked Example

Three hypothetical situations. Descriptions only; this is not a guide to any strategy:

| Situation | Category |
|---|---|
| A salaried person invests in a product the law lists as eligible for a deduction and claims it with correct documents | Tax planning |
| Several entities are created that have no real business purpose, only to shift income and obtain a tax benefit | Possible avoidance; may be disregarded under anti-avoidance rules |
| A person receives rent but does not report it and keeps no records | Evasion: an offence that can bring penalties and prosecution |

## Misconceptions

- "If it saves tax it must be planning." Legality depends on substance and honest reporting, not on the tax saved.
- "Cash transactions are not tracked, so unreported income is safe." Evasion is an offence, and information flows between banks, registrars and the tax authority.
- "Avoidance and evasion are the same." Avoidance exploits the law and can be challenged; evasion breaks it.
- "A scheme that promises to cut tax to nothing is a clever product." Such promises are a warning sign (see "Investor Scams and Unrealistic Returns").
