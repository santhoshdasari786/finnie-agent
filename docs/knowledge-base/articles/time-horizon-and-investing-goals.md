# Time Horizon: Short-Term vs Long-Term Investing

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what a time horizon is, why it changes how much price movement matters, and why a long horizon lowers but does not remove risk, without telling the user what horizon or mix to choose.

## Core Concepts

- The **time horizon** (investing timeframe) is the number of months, years or decades a person plans to invest to reach a financial goal (Investor.gov).
- Investor.gov says investors with a longer time horizon may feel comfortable taking on riskier or more volatile investments, and those with a shorter one may prefer less risky or less volatile investments.
- **Why horizon matters:** prices move up and down in the short run. If money is needed soon, a fall may arrive before it can recover; if it is not needed for many years, there is more time for recovery, though none is guaranteed.
- FINRA notes that historical data suggest holding a broad portfolio of stocks over an extended period significantly reduces the chance of losing principal, but warns against reading that as "no risk": stocks do not get safer the longer you hold them.
- FINRA's example: a portfolio that grew from $10,000 to $20,000 over 19 years and then lost 20 percent in the 20th year ends at $16,000, which is still a gain but less than before the fall.
- FINRA also points out that life events such as a job loss or medical costs can force a person to sell during a downturn, so the planned horizon and the real one can differ.
- **Several goals, several horizons:** a person can have a goal in two years (a down payment) and another in thirty (retirement), and each has its own horizon and its own tolerance for movement.
- A horizon is linked to **liquidity**: money needed on a known date should be available then (see the three pillars article).
- Risk tolerance (how much movement a person is comfortable with) and risk capacity (how much loss a person can afford) are separate from the horizon, and all three interact.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical investment of ₹1,00,000 falls 30% in its first year, to ₹70,000.

- To get back to ₹1,00,000 it must then rise by 100,000 / 70,000 − 1 ≈ 42.9%.
- An investor who needs the money at the end of year one has to accept ₹70,000.
- An investor with ten more years has time to wait, but the recovery is not assured: how long it takes depends on what the investment does.

The figures are illustrative and not a forecast of any investment.

## Misconceptions

- "If I hold long enough, I cannot lose." Long holding periods reduce the chance of loss in historical data but do not eliminate it.
- "Long term means I can ignore everything." Costs, taxes and changes in a person's own situation still matter.
- "Short term is the same as risky." It means less time to recover from a fall, not that every short-term investment is risky.
- "One horizon fits all my money." Different goals have different dates.
