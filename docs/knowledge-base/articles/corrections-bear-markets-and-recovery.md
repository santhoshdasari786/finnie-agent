# Market Corrections, Bear Markets and Recovery

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to define bull and bear markets and corrections, explain that falls come in different sizes and durations, and demonstrate why a percentage loss needs a larger percentage gain to recover, without predicting when any fall will start or end.

## Core Concepts

- SEBI's investor site: "The terms 'bull market' and 'bear market' are used to describe the overall trend of the securities market."
- A **bull market** "is a phase when stock prices are rising and investors are optimistic about the market. This happens when the economy is doing well, businesses are growing quickly... and they can make good profits."
- A **bear market** is a phase of falling prices and a negative outlook, which can follow a weak economy, a recession, geopolitical tension or other shocks (SEBI Investor).
- A **correction** is a shorter and usually smaller fall after a rise; it is described by SEBI's glossary as a temporary reversal of trend. The common figures for the size of a correction or a bear market are conventions: SEBI's pages give none, and the U.S. SEC's Investor.gov is reported to describe a bear market as a fall of 20% or more in a broad index over at least two months (seen in a search summary).
- **Falls differ in size, speed and length.** Some are sharp and brief, others slow and long. Past patterns do not tell when the next one will start or how it will end.
- **Recovery is not automatic or evenly timed.** Broad markets have recovered from past falls, but the time taken has varied, and individual companies, sectors and funds can fail to recover.
- **Recovery arithmetic:** because gains and losses are applied to different bases, a loss of x% needs a gain larger than x% to get back to the starting value. The formula is gain needed = 1 ÷ (1 − loss) − 1.
- **Why it matters:** the deeper the fall, the disproportionately larger the climb back. It is one reason concentration and borrowing are risky (see "Leverage, Margin and Forced Liquidation").
- **Investor tools** (general educational knowledge): holding a mix of assets, keeping emergency savings, matching the time horizon to the investment, and investing regularly, which SEBI notes can help manage volatility.

## Worked Example

Gain needed to return to the starting value after a loss. The arithmetic is exact; the fall itself is hypothetical:

| Loss | Gain needed to recover |
|---|---|
| 10% | 11.1% |
| 20% | 25.0% |
| 30% | 42.9% |
| 40% | 66.7% |
| 50% | 100.0% |
| 75% | 300.0% |

For ₹1,00,000: a 50% fall leaves ₹50,000, and a 100% gain on ₹50,000 is needed to get back to ₹1,00,000. A 20% fall followed by a 20% gain does not recover: ₹1,00,000 → ₹80,000 → ₹96,000. The table says nothing about how likely any of these falls are.

## Misconceptions

- "A 20% fall is recovered by a 20% rise." It needs 25%.
- "Markets always recover quickly." Timing is uncertain and has varied widely.
- "A correction and a bear market are official categories." The labels are conventions used in the market, not regulatory definitions.
- "If I know it is a bear market I can time the bottom." The bottom is clear only afterwards.
