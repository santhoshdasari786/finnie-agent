# SIPs and Periodic Investing

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what an SIP is mechanically, what "rupee-cost averaging" actually does (and its real limitations), and avoid implying SIPs guarantee better outcomes than lump-sum investing.

## Core Concepts

- An **SIP (Systematic Investment Plan)** is an instruction to invest a fixed amount into a chosen mutual fund at regular intervals (daily, weekly, or monthly), automatically, typically via auto-debit. Per AMFI's own investor education material, SIP instalments can be as low as ₹250–₹500 per month — though the exact minimum and frequency options vary by scheme, so confirm the specific fund's terms rather than assuming this figure universally.
- The mechanism behind SIPs is sometimes called **rupee-cost averaging**: investing a fixed amount regularly buys more units when the price/NAV is low and fewer units when it's high, which averages the purchase cost over time compared to investing the same total amount all at once at a single (unknown) price point.
- SIPs don't require timing the market — the amount and frequency are fixed regardless of price, which removes the need to decide "is now a good time to invest?" for each installment.
- **Lump-sum vs. SIP is not a question of which is universally better** — it depends on whether the investor has the money available all at once, and historically neither approach has been proven to always outperform the other (see "Lump-Sum Investing vs. Periodic Investing").
- Returns on a SIP (multiple contributions at different dates) should be measured with **XIRR**, not CAGR — see "CAGR vs. XIRR vs. Absolute Returns."

## Worked Example

AMFI's own illustrative example (from its investor education booklet): a ₹1,000 SIP instalment buys 50 units when the NAV is ₹20, and 100 units when the NAV is ₹10. Total invested: ₹2,000. Total units: 150. Average cost per unit = ₹2,000 ÷ 150 ≈ **₹13.33** — notably lower than the simple average of the two NAVs (₹15), because more units were bought when the price was lower. This is the mechanical effect rupee-cost averaging describes.

*(AMFI's own disclaimer on this example: "The calculations shown are for informational and illustrative purposes only.")*

## Misconceptions

- Rupee-cost averaging is a mechanical description of *how units are accumulated at different prices* — it is not a guarantee of a better overall return than any alternative approach.
- An SIP does not eliminate investment risk — the underlying fund's risk (equity, debt, etc.) still applies to every installment invested.
- "SIP" refers to the investment *mechanism* (fixed amount, fixed interval), not a separate asset class or guaranteed-return product.
