# Index Funds vs. Actively Managed Funds

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain how passive and active funds differ in objective, cost and the kind of risk the investor takes, and say that neither approach is guaranteed to outperform the other, without recommending one.

## Core Concepts

- **Active funds** are those "where fund managers actively select and manage securities based on research and market outlook, with the objective of outperforming a benchmark index" (AMFI booklet). The investor relies on the manager's skill, and the fund's performance can be better or worse than its benchmark.
- **Passive funds** "aim to mirror the performance of a particular market index" and hold "a portfolio that replicates a stated index or benchmark. The fund manager has a passive role" (AMFI). Index funds and ETFs are the main forms.
- AMFI lists three reasons investors choose passive funds: they are **simple and transparent** (you know which index the fund tracks), **rule-based** (decisions follow the index methodology, not market calls) and **cost-efficient** ("lower portfolio management activity can mean lower costs").
- **Index fund:** invests "in securities of a market index in the same weights as the index, with passive management and minor periodic adjustments", with a regulatory cap on its base expense ratio (AMFI booklet; see "Expense Ratios and Fees" for the dated figure).
- **ETF:** "a marketable security that tracks an index, a commodity, bonds, or a basket of assets like an index fund", listed on exchanges, with units held in demat form (AMFI).
- **Tracking difference.** A passive fund will usually trail its index slightly because of costs and small timing gaps; the difference is called tracking error or tracking difference.
- **Risk is different, not absent.** A passive fund takes the full risk of its index: when the index falls, so does the fund. An active fund adds a second risk: that the manager's choices do worse than the index.
- Passive funds suit being compared with a benchmark; active funds are judged by whether they beat one **after costs**, over comparable periods (see "Comparing Investment Products Fairly").

## Worked Example

A hypothetical comparison over one year, using illustrative numbers only:

| | Index fund | Active fund |
|---|---|---|
| Benchmark return | 12% | 12% |
| Gross return before costs | 12% | 13% |
| Expense ratio | 0.2% | 1.0% |
| Net return to investor | about 11.8% | about 12.0% |
| Difference from benchmark | -0.2 points | 0.0 points |

In this made-up year the active fund's extra gross return mostly paid for its higher cost. In another year the active fund might trail by more or beat by more. Active outperformance in one period does not reliably repeat, and the figures above are not a forecast.

## Misconceptions

- "Passive means no risk." An index fund falls with its market.
- "Active funds always beat index funds" or "never beat them." Both claims are too strong; results vary by period, category and fund.
- "Index funds are identical." Funds tracking the same index can differ in cost and tracking difference, and indices differ in what they hold.
- "Lower cost guarantees higher return." Cost is one factor; it is a reliable drag on net return but does not set the gross return.
