# Financial Planning: Net Worth, SMART Goals and Time Horizons

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain the steps of financial planning in SEBI's booklet, show how net worth and SMART goals work with examples, and connect goals to time horizons, without giving any person a specific plan.

## Core Concepts

- **Financial planning** (SEBI booklet) is the process of estimating a person's financial needs and implementing a comprehensive plan to meet them over a lifetime. Examples of needs: the birth of a child, education, buying a house, marriage, and emergencies such as illness, accident or natural calamity.
- **Start with the current position:** understand income, expenses, assets and liabilities. **Net worth** is simply assets owned minus liabilities owed. It indicates capacity to reach goals such as buying a home or paying for education.
- **Goals** can be basic (food, clothing, shelter), secondary or advanced (education, house, marriage), retirement, and estate planning. Goals change over a lifetime, so the plan should be reviewed regularly.
- The booklet's tip: plan a **savings amount first**, then expenses, summed up as income minus savings equals expenditure ("spend what is left after saving").
- **SMART goals:** specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-bound. The booklet contrasts vague statements with better ones, for example "I will save money" versus "I will save ₹48,000 every year by setting aside ₹4,000 every month".
- **Five-step approach:** the booklet presents a five-step approach to achieving goals. The first four steps, which are the ones read for this article, are: (1) identify specific goals; (2) classify them as short, medium or long term; (3) decide the asset allocation; (4) choose investments with diversification within asset classes.
- **Time horizons** in the booklet: short-term goals arise within a few months to a year; medium-term goals have a horizon of one to eight years (such as buying property or starting a venture); long-term goals are eight years or more (such as retirement). The booklet's cut-offs are one way to classify, and other sources may draw the lines differently.
- **Goal-based investing** works out the future value of each goal and uses asset allocation to pursue it, according to age, risk appetite, financial situation and investment horizon.
- Asset allocation and diversification are described in the booklet as tools; the booklet notes diversification does not guarantee against loss.
- A plan is personal. The article describes the method and does not give a plan or a product for anyone.

## Worked Example

The booklet's net worth illustration (hypothetical):

| Assets | ₹ | Liabilities | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Car | 25,000 | House loan | 20,00,000 |
| Bank balance | 5,00,000 | Car loan | 10,000 |
| House | 50,00,000 | | |
| **Total** | **55,25,000** | **Total** | **20,10,000** |

Net worth = 55,25,000 − 20,10,000 = ₹35,15,000.

A SMART version of a vague goal: "I want to save for a holiday" becomes "I will save ₹4,000 every month for 12 months, ₹48,000 in total, to pay for a holiday next December." It is specific (amount), measurable (₹48,000), achievable (₹4,000 a month), realistic (within income) and time-bound (12 months). By the booklet's classification this is a short-term goal.

## Misconceptions

- "Planning is only for the wealthy." The method works at any income; net worth can be small or even negative.
- "A goal like 'be rich' is enough." Without an amount and date it cannot be measured or planned for.
- "Set the plan once." The booklet says goals change and the plan should be reviewed.
- "All goals have the same horizon." Each goal has its own date and so its own tolerance for price movements.
