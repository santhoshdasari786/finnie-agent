# Open-Ended, Close-Ended and Interval Mutual Funds

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain the three structures of mutual fund schemes, what each means for when money can be put in or taken out, and how structure differs from management style and from the type of assets held, without recommending a structure.

## Core Concepts

- AMFI's booklet says mutual funds can be classified in several ways: by **structure**, by **management style** and by **investment objective**, with the type of assets (equity, debt, hybrid and others) described separately.
- **Open-ended funds** allow investors to invest in or redeem units on **all business days at the current NAV**, which gives flexibility and easy access to money.
- **Close-ended funds** have a **fixed maturity period** and can be redeemed only at maturity. Their units are **listed on stock exchanges**, so an investor can sell before maturity if needed (subject to a buyer being available at an acceptable price).
- **Interval funds** allow buying and selling only during **specific periods** decided by the fund. They combine features of open-ended and close-ended funds and are listed on stock exchanges.
- **Management style:** **active funds** are run by managers who select securities from research and market outlook, aiming to beat a benchmark index; **passive funds** replicate a stated index and decisions follow the index, which tends to mean simpler, rule-based and lower-cost management (see the active versus passive article).
- **Investment objective:** AMFI groups schemes by what they aim for, such as capital growth, regular income, liquidity and capital preservation, or tax saving; these map to categories like equity, debt, liquid and tax-saving schemes.
- Structure affects **liquidity**: an open-ended fund lets you transact at NAV on business days, subject to exit loads or lock-ins; a close-ended fund's exit before maturity depends on the exchange price, which can differ from NAV.
- **Lock-ins** apply to certain schemes, for example tax-saving equity schemes and solution-oriented funds, and are a separate feature from the structure.
- A structure label does not tell you the fund's risk; the riskometer, the category and the scheme documents do.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical investor holds three units' worth of ideas.

- In an **open-ended** scheme with a NAV of ₹50, 200 units are redeemed on any business day at that day's NAV: 200 × ₹50 = ₹10,000, less any exit load.
- In a **close-ended** scheme maturing in three years, the investor wants out after one year. They must sell on the exchange to another buyer. If the NAV is ₹50 but buyers offer only ₹46, they receive 200 × ₹46 = ₹9,200.
- In an **interval** scheme that opens a transaction window only during specific periods, an investor who misses the window must wait for the next one or sell on the exchange.

The numbers are invented to show the liquidity difference; actual prices, loads and windows depend on the scheme.

## Misconceptions

- "Close-ended means I cannot sell until maturity." Units are listed, so they can be sold on the exchange, though at a market price that may differ from NAV.
- "Open-ended means no restrictions." Exit loads, lock-ins and cut-off times can apply.
- "Structure decides risk." It affects liquidity; risk depends on what the fund holds.
- "Interval funds are the same as open-ended funds." Transactions are allowed only in specified periods.
