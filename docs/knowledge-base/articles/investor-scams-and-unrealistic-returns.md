# Common Investment Scams and Unrealistic Return Promises

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to list common warning signs of investment fraud and explain *why* each one is a red flag, grounded in how legitimate markets and regulation actually work — without naming or accusing any specific real company/scheme.

## Core Concepts

- **"Guaranteed" high returns are themselves a red flag** — see "Understanding Risk and Return": return and uncertainty are linked, and any investment claiming high returns with no risk is describing something that doesn't match how markets work.
- **Pressure tactics** (urgency: "offer closes today," secrecy: "don't tell anyone," exclusivity: "only for people like you") are common across many fraud patterns — legitimate investment opportunities don't typically require instant, secretive decisions.
- **Unregistered or unverifiable intermediaries**: legitimate entities offering securities/investment advice in India are generally expected to be registered with the relevant regulator (SEBI, for market intermediaries) — see "How to Verify a Financial Intermediary."
- **Complex or vague explanations of how returns are generated** — if the underlying business/strategy genuinely can't be explained clearly, that's a reason for caution, not reassurance.
- **Referral-driven structures** where returns depend primarily on recruiting more investors (rather than an underlying investment/business activity) resemble Ponzi/pyramid patterns, which are structurally unsustainable regardless of claimed legitimacy.

## Worked Example (Pattern Recognition, Not a Real Case)

A hypothetical, illustrative pattern combining common red flags:

> "Invest ₹50,000 and get a guaranteed 5% *per month*, paid out immediately, from a strategy we can't fully explain due to 'proprietary methods.' Spots are limited — decide within 24 hours, and don't discuss details publicly."

Red flags present: guaranteed return language, implausibly high rate (5%/month compounds to a return pattern far outside what regulated markets have historically sustained), vague/unexplainable mechanism, urgency pressure, secrecy request. None of these individually proves fraud, but this combination is a well-known pattern worth treating with serious skepticism.

## Misconceptions

- A scheme having a professional-looking website, testimonials, or even some initial on-time payouts does not make it legitimate — early payouts are a common tactic to build trust before a larger failure.
- "I know someone who got paid" is not reliable evidence a scheme is legitimate — fraud schemes frequently pay early participants, funded by later participants' contributions, until the structure collapses.
- Regulation reduces but does not eliminate the possibility of fraud — registration/verification is a useful check, not an absolute guarantee.
