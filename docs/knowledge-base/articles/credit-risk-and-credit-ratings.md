# Credit Risk and Credit Ratings

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain credit (default) risk, describe what a credit rating is and is not, read the basic rating scale, and explain why debt with a higher rating still carries risk, without recommending any bond, fund or issuer.

## Core Concepts

- **Credit risk** is the chance that a borrower will not pay interest or repay principal in full and on time. Lending money through a bond, debenture or deposit means taking this risk. Government securities issued by the Government of India are "free from risk of default (credit risk)" because they are sovereign borrowing (SEBI Beginner's Guide).
- A **credit rating** "represents the rating agency's opinion on the likelihood of a rated debt obligation being repaid in full and on time. Credit rating is therefore an assessment of the probability of default" (SEBI FAQs on Credit Rating Agencies, March 2026).
- **What a rating does not say:** "Credit ratings are not a recommendation to buy, hold or sell a debt instrument." A rating "merely provides an additional input to the investor", who must still do an independent analysis (same FAQ). Ratings do not measure other factors, such as price risk or liquidity.
- **The scale.** SEBI standardises long-term rating symbols, each prefixed by the agency's name: **AAA** (highest degree of safety, lowest credit risk), **AA** (very low credit risk), **A** (adequate safety), **BBB** (moderate credit risk), **BB** (moderate risk of default), **B** (high risk of default), **C** (very high risk of default) and **D** (in default or expected to be in default soon). Plus and minus modifiers show standing within a category.
- **Investment grade** is commonly used for the upper part of the scale (BBB- and above); below that is called speculative grade (stated in a SEBI-linked summary found by search; not on the FAQ page text read here).
- Ratings are **opinions at a point in time** and can be revised up or down; SEBI's regulations require agencies to state that ratings are not buy, hold or sell recommendations.
- **Higher yield usually signals higher credit risk.** SEBI's guide notes corporate debentures may offer "a higher rate of interest than bank fixed deposit, because of the higher risk".
- Debt mutual funds carry the credit risk of the bonds they hold; funds may restrict themselves to highly rated bonds (for example, a corporate bond fund category requires high-rated bonds, per AMFI).

## Worked Example

Two hypothetical bonds, both promising to repay ₹1,000 in 3 years. Illustration only:

| | Bond P | Bond Q |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer's rating | AAA | BB |
| Interest offered | Lower | Higher |
| What the rating suggests | Very low probability of default | A meaningfully higher probability of default |
| If the issuer defaults | Possible but unlikely | More likely |

Bond Q pays more *because* investors demand compensation for the extra risk of not being repaid. The higher interest is a price for risk, not a bonus.

## Misconceptions

- "AAA means the investment cannot lose money." A top rating means the lowest assessed credit risk, not zero. Highly rated issuers have defaulted in India's history (a general-knowledge point, not from the SEBI FAQ), and a rating can be lowered before or after a problem.
- "A rating is advice from the regulator." The rating is the agency's opinion; SEBI registers and supervises rating agencies but does not endorse a rating.
- "Rated means safe from price falls." A highly rated bond's price can still fall when interest rates rise (see "Bond Prices and Interest-Rate Risk").
- "Higher interest on an FD or bond is simply better." Ask what extra risk is being taken for it.
