# Circuit Breakers: Why Trading Is Sometimes Halted

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what a market-wide circuit breaker is, how the NSE describes its stages, and what a halt means for an investor, while saying that the exact levels and timings are set by regulators and can change.

## Core Concepts

- A **circuit breaker** is a rule that pauses trading when prices move too far, too fast, to give participants time to take in information and avoid panic-driven trading.
- The NSE page describes an **index-based market-wide circuit breaker** that has existed in India since 2001 under a SEBI circular, with the rules partly modified by a SEBI circular in 2013.
- It applies at **three stages** of index movement, in either direction. When triggered, it brings about a **coordinated trading halt in all equity and equity derivative markets nationwide**.
- It is triggered by movement in either the **BSE Sensex or the Nifty 50**, whichever is breached earlier.
- The **length of the halt depends on the stage and on the time of day** the trigger happens. At the first stage a breach late in the day leads to no halt or a shorter one; at the third stage the market stays closed for the rest of the day.
- After a halt, the market **reopens with a pre-open call auction session**, a short period in which orders are collected and a price is set before normal trading resumes.
- The trigger levels are computed **every day from the previous day's closing level** of the index, rounded to the nearest tick size, so the actual index points change daily.
- There are also **price bands on individual stocks** (daily limits on how far a stock can move). The page read for this article describes the market-wide system; individual band rules were not read here.
- A halt **stops trading, not the underlying fall**. It does not protect the value of holdings, and prices can resume moving, up or down, when trading restarts.
- Exact percentages, durations and clock times are published by the exchange and SEBI and have been revised before. Verify the current table on the exchange website before quoting any of them.

## Worked Example

The NSE page, as read on 2026-11-18, gives this table for the market-wide system (verify before relying on it):

| Fall in index | Trigger time | Halt | Pre-open session |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | before 1:00 pm | 45 minutes | 15 minutes |
| 10% | 1:00 pm to 2:30 pm | 15 minutes | 15 minutes |
| 10% | after 2:30 pm | no halt | not applicable |
| 15% | before 1:00 pm | 1 hour 45 minutes | 15 minutes |
| 15% | 1:00 pm to 2:00 pm | 45 minutes | 15 minutes |
| 15% | after 2:00 pm | rest of the day | not applicable |
| 20% | any time | rest of the day | not applicable |

A hypothetical illustration: if the index closed yesterday at 24,000, the stage levels for today would be 10% away = 21,600, 15% = 20,400 and 20% = 19,200 (for a fall). If the index touches 21,600 at 11:00 am, trading halts for 45 minutes, followed by a 15-minute pre-open session. The index level is invented.

## Misconceptions

- "A circuit breaker protects my money." It pauses trading; prices can keep falling afterwards.
- "Trading halts only happen in crashes." Market-wide halts are for very large index moves; stock-level limits work differently.
- "The levels are fixed forever." They have been changed by SEBI and the exchanges before.
- "A halt means the market has bottomed." It says nothing about where prices go next.
