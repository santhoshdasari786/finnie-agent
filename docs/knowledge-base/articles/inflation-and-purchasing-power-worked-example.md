# Inflation and Purchasing Power: A Worked Example

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to show, with an explicit assumed inflation rate, what a fixed sum will be worth in future money, what future prices may look like, and how to convert a nominal return into a real return, while stating that the rate is an assumption and not a forecast.

## Core Concepts

- **Inflation** is a rise in the general level of prices, so the same amount of money buys less over time. FINRA describes the related inflation risk as an investment not earning enough to keep pace with the increasing cost of living.
- **Future price of a fixed basket:** price today × (1 + inflation)^years.
- **Purchasing power of a fixed sum:** amount ÷ (1 + inflation)^years, measured in today's money.
- **Real return** is the return after allowing for inflation: (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. The quick approximation, nominal minus inflation, is close but not exact.
- The assumed inflation rate is a choice made for the illustration. Actual inflation varies from year to year, and the right rate to assume for a given goal depends on what is being bought.
- Different goals face different price rises: education or medical costs may rise faster or slower than the general average.
- Because the effect compounds, it grows with time: the longer the period, the larger the gap between a sum's face value and what it buys.
- Taxes and fees reduce returns further and are ignored here to isolate inflation.

## Worked Example

Assumptions (hypothetical): inflation of 5% a year, taken as constant; ₹1,00,000 today.

**1. What money will buy.** Cash kept idle:
- After 10 years it buys what ₹61,391 buys today (1,00,000 ÷ 1.05^10).
- After 20 years it buys what ₹37,689 buys today.

**2. What things will cost.** Something that costs ₹1,00,000 today would cost about ₹1,62,889 in 10 years and ₹2,65,330 in 20 years (1,00,000 × 1.05^n).

**3. A nominal return versus a real one.** Suppose the ₹1,00,000 earns 7% a year before tax.
- The nominal value is ₹1,96,715 after 10 years and ₹3,86,968 after 20.
- The real return is 1.07 ÷ 1.05 − 1 ≈ 1.905% a year, not exactly 2%.
- In today's purchasing power, the money is worth ₹1,20,766 after 10 years and ₹1,45,844 after 20, far less than the nominal figures suggest.

Rounded to the nearest rupee. The 5% and 7% are assumptions for the illustration.

## Misconceptions

- "My balance grew, so I am richer." Nominal growth below inflation is a loss of purchasing power.
- "Inflation only matters for the long term." It also affects money held for a few years, though the effect is smaller.
- "Real return is nominal minus inflation, exactly." That is a close approximation; the exact method divides.
- "Inflation will be the number used in the example." It is a simple assumption; actual inflation changes.
