# Types of Investment Risk: Market, Company, Inflation, Liquidity and More

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to name the main kinds of investment risk, explain each in plain words with an example, and say which ones diversification can reduce, without telling the user which risks to accept.

## Core Concepts

SEBI's Financial Education Booklet lists seven key risks to analyse before investing:

- **Market (systematic) risk:** losses from factors that affect the overall performance of financial markets and the economy. Stock market bubbles and crashes are examples. It affects most investments at once and cannot be removed by owning more of the same market.
- **Unsystematic risk:** uncertainty specific to a company or industry, such as a new competitor taking market share, a regulatory change that hurts sales, a change in management or a product recall. Spreading investments across companies and industries reduces it.
- **Inflation risk (purchasing power risk):** the chance that an investment's cash flows will be worth less in the future because inflation reduces what money buys.
- **Liquidity risk:** the investment cannot be bought, sold or converted to cash quickly enough to prevent or limit a loss or meet an immediate expense. SEBI notes diversifying can reduce this risk to a great extent.
- **Business risk:** the risk that a business stops operating because of an unfavourable market or financial situation.
- **Volatility risk:** the risk that a company's stock price fluctuates, even when the company is not in danger of failing.
- **Currency risk:** the possibility of losing money because of unfavourable movements in exchange rates; it affects investors with assets across borders.
- Other risks named elsewhere in these materials include **interest-rate risk and credit (default) risk** for bonds and debt funds, and **concentration risk**, having too much in one holding.
- SEBI's booklet suggests ways to mitigate risk: **asset allocation** and diversification across sectors and companies, investing through a **systematic investment plan** or in smaller lots over time to manage volatility, understanding a company's fundamentals, and ignoring rumours and unsolicited messages.
- Risks overlap, and reducing one can raise another: a very safe, liquid deposit has little volatility but is exposed to inflation risk.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical person holds only shares of one company, a manufacturer that exports.

- The whole stock market falls sharply: **market risk**; it would hit most shares.
- A rival launches a cheaper product and the company loses customers: **unsystematic risk**; it affects this company, not the whole market.
- The company's share price swings widely from month to month although its business is sound: **volatility risk**.
- The rupee strengthens, reducing what the company earns on exports: a **currency** effect on this business.
- The person needs cash in a week, but few buyers are quoting the share: **liquidity risk**.
- Holding other companies and asset classes as well would reduce the unsystematic and concentration risk, but not the market risk.

The scenario is invented to show the labels.

## Misconceptions

- "Diversification removes all risk." It mainly reduces company-specific risk; market-wide risk remains.
- "Risk means only losing money in a crash." It also includes losing purchasing power, being unable to sell, and exchange-rate moves.
- "A safe investment has no risk." It usually swaps one risk for another, such as inflation or liquidity risk.
- "Volatility is the same as failure." Prices can swing even when a company is healthy; business risk is about the company's survival.
