# Pre-Tax vs. Post-Tax Returns

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain the difference between the return an investment advertises and the return the investor keeps after tax and charges, show the arithmetic with clearly hypothetical numbers, and explain that tax treatment differs by product, without stating current tax rates.

## Core Concepts

- A **pre-tax return** is the return before the investor's taxes. Most advertised rates (an FD's interest rate, a fund's past return) are pre-tax.
- A **post-tax return** is what remains after the taxes the investor actually pays on that income or gain. Because products are taxed differently, two products with the same pre-tax return can leave the investor with different amounts.
- **Different products, different tax events.** Interest on deposits is generally taxed as it accrues. Dividends are taxed when received. Gains on shares and funds are taxed when sold, with treatment depending on holding period and asset type (see "Capital Gains Tax Basics in India" and "Dividend Income and Taxation"). Timing matters: tax paid later lets more money keep compounding in the meantime.
- **Tax depends on the person.** The same interest is taxed at different rates for different taxpayers, depending on their income and the tax regime they choose.
- **Real return** goes one step further: after tax, subtract inflation to see the change in purchasing power. SEBI's Financial Education Booklet advises determining your "real rate of return, or the return you can expect after factoring in the effects of inflation".
- **Charges** (expense ratios, brokerage, exit loads) also reduce what the investor keeps (see "Expense Ratios and Fees").
- When comparing products, compare **like with like**: after tax, after charges, over the same period (see "How to Compare Two Investment Products Fairly").
- Tax rules **change**, so a comparison done today may not hold under next year's rules. Use current official guidance for the actual figures.

## Worked Example

All figures are assumed to show the method. They are not current rates.

- A deposit pays **8% interest** a year (pre-tax).
- Assume the investor's interest is taxed at **30%** (an illustrative rate). Tax per ₹100 of interest: ₹30. The investor keeps ₹70, so the post-tax return is **5.6%** (8% × 0.70).
- If inflation is **5%**, the real post-tax return is about (1.056 ÷ 1.05) − 1 = **0.57%**. In purchasing power the investor has barely moved ahead.

Now suppose the same investor compares a second product with a pre-tax return of 6.5% that is taxed more lightly, at an assumed 10%. Its post-tax return is 6.5% × 0.90 = **5.85%**. In this made-up case the second product, with the lower headline return, leaves slightly more after tax. A different tax rate would reverse the result, which is exactly why the headline number alone is not enough.

## Misconceptions

- "The higher advertised rate always wins." The comparison belongs after tax, charges and inflation.
- "Tax only matters when I sell." Some income (deposit interest, dividends) is taxed as it is earned.
- "Everyone is taxed at the same rate on the same product." The rate depends on the individual's income and regime.
- "A tax-saving product is automatically the better product." A deduction is one factor; liquidity, risk and return still matter.
