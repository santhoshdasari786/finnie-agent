# Unauthorised Digital Transactions: What to Do and When You Are Liable

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to list basic safe digital-payment habits, explain why reporting quickly matters, and describe how the SEBI booklet summarises RBI's liability rules, while telling the user to confirm the current rules with the bank and RBI.

## Core Concepts

- SEBI's Financial Education Booklet lists **do's and don'ts for digital payments**. Do: use passwords on computers and phones and change them and the security settings regularly; visit the bank's secure internet banking site directly; avoid internet banking on unsecured public Wi-Fi; log out right after a transaction; update apps and the phone's security software; and **check balances and transaction history regularly**.
- Don't: save mobile banking login details on the phone; leave a phone logged in or unattended during a transaction; leave a PC lying around or let a stranger use it; download apps from untrustworthy sources.
- **Report quickly.** The booklet advises that if you suspect an unauthorised transaction, report it to the bank immediately, or at least within three working days, so that the complaint is addressed in your favour.
- **Liability, as the booklet describes RBI's position:** a customer has **no liability** when an unauthorised transaction happens because of fraud, contributory negligence or deficiency on the bank's part. Even when neither the customer nor the bank is at fault and the problem is elsewhere in the system, the customer has no liability **provided the bank is told within three days of receiving the alert**, and the bank credits the amount within a specified period.
- Where the fraud happens because of the **customer's negligence**, such as sharing credentials, the **entire loss until the date of reporting** is borne by the customer.
- RBI has told banks to send **SMS and email alerts immediately** after a transaction, enabled so the customer can reply to report a fraud.
- Therefore the mobile number and email registered with the bank must be current, and alerts must be read.
- Scammers often try to get the **one-time password (OTP), PIN or a remote-access app installed**. A bank will not ask for them, and sharing them can count as customer negligence.
- These are the booklet's descriptions of rules around 2020. RBI's rules on customer liability and timelines have been updated before; check the bank's current policy and RBI's circular.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical customer receives an SMS alert for a payment they did not make.

1. They read the alert and immediately call the bank's official number (from its website or card), asking to block the card or account, and report the transaction in writing as well.
2. They report the same day, which is within the three-day window the booklet describes, and keep the complaint reference number.
3. They had not shared their OTP or PIN with anyone. Under the booklet's summary of RBI's rules, the customer has no liability if the matter is reported on time and the problem was not their negligence, and the bank is to credit the amount within a specified period.
4. If the bank does not resolve it, they use the bank's grievance process and the banking ombudsman channels in the grievance article.

The scenario is invented to show the sequence and does not guarantee any particular outcome.

## Misconceptions

- "If it was a fraud, the bank always pays." Under the booklet's summary, loss caused by the customer's negligence is borne by the customer until reported.
- "I can report it next week." The booklet stresses reporting immediately, within three working days at most.
- "A bank officer may ask for my OTP to fix it." Genuine banks do not ask for OTPs or PINs.
- "Alerts are noise." They are what starts the reporting clock and shows a problem early.
