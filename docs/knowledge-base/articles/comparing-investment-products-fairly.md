# How to Compare Two Investment Products Fairly

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to walk a user through a structured, multi-dimensional comparison of two investment products, rather than reducing the comparison to a single headline return number.

## Core Concepts

A fair comparison checks all of the following dimensions — comparing only one (usually "which has the higher return?") is the most common way comparisons mislead:

- **Risk profile** — are the two products exposed to comparable risk (see "Understanding Risk and Return")? Comparing a government-backed fixed deposit's return to an equity fund's return without acknowledging the very different risk levels isn't a fair comparison.
- **Fees** — expense ratio, brokerage, exit loads, account/platform fees (see "Expense Ratios and the Impact of Fees") — compare *net* return potential, not just the headline rate.
- **Liquidity** — how easily and at what cost can the investment be exited before its natural maturity/horizon? A product with a long lock-in is not directly comparable to one with daily liquidity, even at the same headline return.
- **Taxation** — two products can have identical pre-tax returns and different after-tax outcomes, because tax treatment depends on instrument type and holding period (see "Capital Gains Tax Basics in India" and "Pre-Tax vs. Post-Tax Returns"). Always compare returns *after* accounting for applicable taxes where possible.
- **Tenure/holding period** — compare returns over the *same* time period using the *same* measure (see "CAGR vs. XIRR vs. Absolute Returns") — comparing a 1-year return on one product to a 5-year CAGR on another is not meaningful.
- **What the return figure actually represents** — gross or net of fees? Pre-tax or post-tax? Backward-looking historical or a projected/illustrative assumption?

## Worked Example

A simplified comparison checklist applied to two illustrative (not real) products:

| Dimension | Product A (illustrative) | Product B (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Stated return | 8% p.a. | 10% p.a. |
| Risk level | Lower (debt-oriented) | Higher (equity-oriented) |
| Expense ratio | 0.5% | 1.5% |
| Lock-in | None | 3 years |
| Tax treatment | Taxed as per holding-period rules for its asset type | Taxed as per holding-period rules for its asset type (likely different from A) |

Looking only at "8% vs. 10%" would favor Product B — but once risk, fees, liquidity, and tax treatment are accounted for, the genuinely comparable picture may look quite different. The point of this framework is to make that fuller picture explicit, not to declare either product "better" in the abstract.

## Misconceptions

- The product with the higher headline return is not automatically the "better" choice — it may carry meaningfully more risk, less liquidity, or less favorable tax treatment.
- Comparing returns over different time periods (one product's 1-year figure vs. another's 5-year figure) produces a misleading comparison even if both numbers are accurate in isolation.
- A fair comparison framework doesn't produce a universal "winner" — the right product depends on the individual's goals, horizon, and risk capacity, which is why this agent stays descriptive (explaining the framework) rather than prescriptive (telling someone which to pick).
