# Exit Loads, Lock-ins and Withdrawal Restrictions

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain the difference between an exit load, a lock-in and an ordinary withdrawal restriction, give examples of where each appears, and explain why liquidity terms matter before investing, without naming any specific scheme's current terms.

## Core Concepts

- **Exit load:** "a fee charged when you redeem mutual fund units and is deducted from the NAV at the time of redemption" (AMFI booklet). It is a percentage of the redemption value and is often set to fall away after a stated holding period; the exact terms are in each scheme's documents.
- **Lock-in:** a period during which the investor *cannot* redeem at all. It is different from an exit load, which lets you sell but charges for it.
- **Mutual fund lock-ins:** an ELSS fund has a 3-year lock-in (AMFI booklet, noting its eligibility for a deduction applies under the old tax regime). Retirement funds and children's funds have a lock-in of at least five years or until a milestone, whichever is earlier.
- **Bank products:** a tax-saver FD is a term deposit with a 5-year lock-in, "no premature withdrawal/loan allowed" (SEBI Financial Education Booklet). An ordinary FD can usually be closed early at a reduced interest rate or with a penalty set by the bank.
- **Government savings schemes** such as the PPF have restrictions on when part-withdrawals and loans are allowed, and a fixed maturity (see "PPF, EPF and NPS").
- **Why they exist:** exit loads discourage short-term trading in schemes meant for the long term, and protect the remaining investors from the cost of frequent redemptions; lock-ins are often the price of a tax benefit or a regulated product's purpose.
- **Liquidity is a cost, too.** An investment you cannot easily get out of must be judged for how likely you are to need the money (see "Saving vs. Investing vs. Speculating").
- **Where to look:** the scheme's offer document, the factsheet or the product's terms and conditions state load, lock-in and withdrawal rules. They can change, so check the current document before investing.

## Worked Example

AMFI's own illustration of an exit load: if the NAV is ₹100 and the exit load is 1%, the redemption price would be ₹99 per unit.

Extending it with hypothetical numbers:

- An investor holds 1,000 units at a NAV of ₹100: value ₹1,00,000.
- If a 1% exit load applies, redeeming everything returns ₹99,000, so the load costs ₹1,000.
- If the scheme's load applies only for the first year and the investor redeems after 13 months, no load is charged and the investor receives ₹1,00,000 (before any tax).

For a 3-year lock-in, none of this applies in the first three years: the units simply cannot be redeemed.

## Misconceptions

- "An exit load and a lock-in are the same thing." One charges a fee to leave, the other blocks leaving.
- "A lock-in guarantees a better return." It restricts access; it does not change the return.
- "Exit loads apply forever." They typically apply for a defined period after investment, but the period differs by scheme.
- "I can always get my money out of anything I own." Liquidity varies by product, and some cannot be sold early at all.
