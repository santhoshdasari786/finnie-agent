# Estate Planning: Wills, Nominees and Powers of Attorney

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what estate planning covers, how a will, a nominee and a power of attorney differ, and why none of them substitutes for another, while stating that succession law is specialised and a qualified lawyer should be consulted for a real case.

## Core Concepts

- **Estate planning** is planning, during one's lifetime, how assets will go to beneficiaries (family, loved ones, institutions) so they can be used or claimed after death with ease and without significant cost (SEBI booklet).
- A **will** is a written legal declaration of how a person wants their assets distributed after death. The booklet says making one is a simple process, and that it can help avoid disputes among beneficiaries.
- **Making a will, as the booklet outlines:** list movable and immovable assets; decide who gets what, wholly or partly; name the person who will carry out (execute) the will; ideally have it witnessed, which adds authenticity; registration is not compulsory but preferable; it can be written on plain paper or prepared with a lawyer.
- **Without a will** (booklet): assets may not go to whom or in the amounts the person would have wanted; distribution follows personal laws and relationships; it can lead to expensive litigation, and succession disputes can take years and reduce the value of what is inherited.
- A **nominee** is, per the booklet, a person who has the right to **custody** of the money on the account holder's death, but is **only a custodian and does not have ownership rights**. Without nomination, it can be costly and slow for legal heirs to get the money. The booklet's example: a friend nominated on a bank FD receives the money but must transfer it to the legal heirs, otherwise the heirs may have to claim it in court.
- A **power of attorney** is a legal document that lets another person act on your behalf while you are alive, for example to access bank accounts or sell a vehicle. A **general** power gives wide authority; a **special** power gives limited authority for a specific transaction. It is not a way to pass assets after death.
- **After a death** (booklet): legal heirs approach banks, depositories, mutual fund companies and insurers with the death certificate and a legal heir certificate to transfer the assets; if there is a will, assets go according to it.
- Rules on succession change and differ by case, so verify them with a qualified lawyer. Taxes, the validity of a will, joint ownership and succession rules for different communities are legal questions this article does not cover.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical person has a bank FD, a demat account and a flat. They want each to reach the right people.

- A **will** states who inherits each asset. 
- **Nominations** on the FD and demat account name who may collect those assets quickly; the booklet says the nominee is a custodian and the money belongs to the legal heirs (as the will or law decides).
- A **power of attorney** given to a relative lets that relative handle certain tasks while the person is alive and unable to, but it ends on death.
- The person keeps a list of accounts and tells a trusted person where it is, so that nothing is missed (see the unclaimed assets article).

The example shows the roles and does not give legal advice.

## Misconceptions

- "A nominee inherits everything." The booklet describes a nominee as a custodian without ownership rights.
- "A will is only for rich people." The booklet describes it as a simple process and lists the problems without one for anyone with assets.
- "A power of attorney lets someone manage my estate after I die." It operates in the person's lifetime.
- "Nomination and a will always say the same thing." They are separate documents and can conflict, so they should be kept consistent.
