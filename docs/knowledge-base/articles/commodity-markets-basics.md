# Commodity Markets Basics: Commodities, Price Risk and Commodity Derivative Exchanges

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what a commodity is, why commodity prices are uncertain, who faces price risk, and what a commodity derivative exchange does, without explaining how to trade or recommending any commodity.

## Core Concepts

- SEBI's Financial Education Booklet defines **commodities** as goods with economic value: the earth's natural products that are produced and traded, usually raw materials for further processing.
- It groups them as **agricultural commodities** (food and non-food crops), **non-agricultural commodities** (metals, energy, polymers and so on) and others (for example cattle and processed foods). Major groups it lists include edible oilseeds, food grains, bullion, metals and energy, spices and fibres.
- **Commodity price risk** is the price uncertainty that hurts those who use or produce a commodity. A manufacturer is hurt when the price of an input such as steel rises; a farmer can be hurt when crop prices are low in one year and the farmer plants less, then prices rise the next year and the profitable crop is missed.
- **What moves commodity prices:** political and regulatory changes, seasonal variations, weather, technology and market conditions (SEBI booklet).
- **Who faces risk:** producers such as farmers, users such as manufacturers, traders and investors, each with different exposures; the booklet lists the risks faced by various stakeholders, especially farmers.
- **Commodity derivative exchanges** are the regulated markets where contracts linked to commodity prices are traded; SEBI's booklet has sections on the exchanges and on the benefits of having them (those passages were only skimmed for this article). Trading through them is **leveraged and risky** for retail participants; see the leverage article.
- **Gold** is the commodity most households meet; see the gold article for the ways to hold it.
- Commodities do not produce income such as interest or dividends; returns come from price changes, which can be large in either direction.
- A commodity's price can respond to inflation, supply shocks or currency moves, so it can behave differently from shares and bonds, but that does not make it safe or a guaranteed hedge.
- Rules for commodity exchanges, contract types and investor eligibility were not covered by the passages read, and have changed over time.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical farmer expects to harvest 100 quintals of a crop in three months and today's price is ₹2,000 a quintal, an expected ₹2,00,000.

- If the price at harvest falls 15% to ₹1,700, the crop sells for ₹1,70,000: a ₹30,000 shortfall. That is **commodity price risk** for a producer.
- A flour mill that buys the same crop faces the opposite risk: a price rise to ₹2,300 raises its cost by ₹30,000 on 100 quintals.
- A producer or user who locks in a price in advance through a regulated contract removes this uncertainty, at the cost of giving up gains from favourable moves. A speculator who takes the opposite side takes on the uncertainty and can lose heavily.

The prices and the quantity are invented.

## Misconceptions

- "Commodity prices only go up with inflation." They can rise or fall sharply for reasons such as weather and supply.
- "Commodities are a safe hedge." They can diversify, but they are volatile and carry no guarantee.
- "Commodity trading is like buying a share." It usually involves leveraged contracts and expiry dates, which add risk.
- "Only farmers care about commodity prices." Manufacturers, consumers and investors are all affected.
