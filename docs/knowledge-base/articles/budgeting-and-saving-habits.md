# Budgeting and Saving Habits: Income, Expenses and Paying Yourself First

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain what a budget is, why it comes before investing, how "save first" works, and walk through a simple budget with assumed numbers, without telling a user how much to save or spend.

## Core Concepts

- **Budgeting** is, in SEBI's Beginner's Guide, the first step in financial planning: a process for tracking, planning and controlling the inflow and outflow of income. It means identifying all sources of income and taking into account current and future expenses, with the aim of meeting financial goals.
- The primary aim of a budget is to ensure **reasonable savings after providing for all expenses**.
- SEBI lists the benefits: it puts **checks and balances** in place to prevent overspending; it takes into account **unexpected needs for funds**; it **disciplines** earning and spending; and it helps maintain the same standard of living **after retirement**.
- **Saving** is the excess of income over expenditure. It usually sits in a savings account or cash, which is very safe and liquid but earns a small rate of interest. **Investing** is setting money aside for longer-term goals, where values rise and fall (SEBI Beginner's Guide).
- **Save first:** SEBI's Financial Education Booklet says to plan a savings amount first and then plan expenses, so that income minus savings equals expenditure, rather than saving whatever is left at the end of the month.
- A budget should include **irregular and unexpected costs** (medical bills, repairs, annual premiums), which is why a buffer of readily available money is useful. How large a buffer to hold depends on the person and is not set here.
- A budget should be **reviewed** as income, prices and goals change; the booklet says the financial plan should be reviewed regularly.
- Budgeting connects to the other pillars: a goal needs a specific saving amount (see the SMART goals article), and **inflation** means the same spending will cost more later.
- Tracking can be done on paper, in a spreadsheet or an app; the method matters less than doing it consistently.

## Worked Example

A hypothetical household earns ₹60,000 a month and uses the save-first approach with assumed amounts:

| Item | ₹ a month |
|---|---|
| Income | 60,000 |
| Planned saving (set aside first) | 9,000 |
| Rent and utilities | 20,000 |
| Food and household | 12,000 |
| Transport | 4,000 |
| Insurance premiums (monthly equivalent) | 3,000 |
| Education and loan repayments | 6,000 |
| Other, including a small buffer for surprises | 6,000 |
| **Total spending** | **51,000** |

Income 60,000 − saving 9,000 = 51,000 available for spending, and the spending lines add up to exactly that. If one month a repair costs ₹8,000 more, the "other" line is overspent and the household checks which line to trim or draws on the buffer, instead of cutting the saving by default.

All figures are invented to show the method; they are not a recommended split.

## Misconceptions

- "Budgeting means cutting out everything enjoyable." It is a plan for where money goes, including what a person chooses to spend on.
- "I will save what is left." Often nothing is left; planning the saving first changes that.
- "A budget is only for people with low incomes." The method works at any income.
- "Once set, a budget stays right." Income, prices and goals change.
