# Understanding Risk and Return

## Purpose

After reading this, the agent should be able to explain why higher potential returns generally come with higher uncertainty, distinguish a few common types of investment risk, and avoid implying that any return is "guaranteed."

## Core Concepts

- **Risk**, in investing, usually means the uncertainty or variability of returns — not simply "chance of losing money," though that's part of it.
- Asset classes with historically higher average returns (e.g. equities) have also historically shown greater short-term variability than lower-return asset classes (e.g. government bonds, fixed deposits) — this relationship is a general pattern, not a guarantee for any specific period.
- Common risk types: **market risk** (prices move with the broader market), **credit risk** (a borrower/issuer fails to pay), **liquidity risk** (can't sell/exit when needed), **interest-rate risk** (bond prices move as rates change), **inflation risk** (returns don't keep up with inflation). AMFI's own investor education booklet frames the "three risks you must be aware of" as market risk, loss of capital, and liquidity risk, with interest-rate risk, credit/default risk, and concentration risk listed as additional risks specific to mutual funds — a slightly different grouping than the list above, but covering the same underlying ideas.
- "Risk-free" is a theoretical concept (often approximated by short-term government securities) — in practice, almost every investment carries at least one of the risk types above.
- A longer time horizon can change how *relevant* short-term volatility is to an investor's goal, but it does not eliminate risk.

## Worked Example

Illustrative framing only, not a prediction:

| Asset class (illustrative) | Typical historical return pattern | Typical historical short-term variability |
|---|---|---|
| Government-backed fixed deposit | Lower | Lower |
| Corporate bonds | Moderate | Moderate |
| Equities (diversified) | Higher (long-run average) | Higher (year-to-year) |

This table describes a general historical *pattern*, not a rule, and not a forecast for any specific product or period.

## Misconceptions

- "Low risk" does not mean "zero risk" — even government-backed instruments carry inflation risk and, in theory, risks related to the issuing government's solvency.
- Higher historical average returns do not mean a specific investment is guaranteed to perform well over any specific future period.
- Risk tolerance (emotional comfort with volatility) and risk capacity (financial ability to absorb a loss) are different things — see "Risk Tolerance vs. Risk Capacity" for that distinction.
